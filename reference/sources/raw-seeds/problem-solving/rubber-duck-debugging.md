Code debugging method

[![](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Rubber_duck_assisting_with_debugging.jpg/250px-Rubber_duck_assisting_with_debugging.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rubber_duck_assisting_with_debugging.jpg)

A [rubber duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_duck "Rubber duck") in use by a developer to aid debugging

**Rubber duck debugging** (or **rubberducking**) is a [debugging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debugging "Debugging") technique in [software engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_engineering "Software engineering"), wherein a [programmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programmer "Programmer") explains their code, step by step, in [natural language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language "Natural language")—either aloud or in writing—to reveal mistakes and misunderstandings.[[1]](#cite_note-1)

Definition
----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Rubber_duck_debugging&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Definition")]

The name is a reference to an anecdote told in *[The Pragmatic Programmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pragmatic_Programmer "The Pragmatic Programmer")* by [Andy Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Hunt_(author) "Andy Hunt (author)") and [Dave Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dave_Thomas_(programmer) "Dave Thomas (programmer)"). The book described rubber ducking as the method of explaining a problem to another party who do not "need to say a word; the simple act of explaining, step by step, what the code is supposed to do often causes the problem to leap off the screen and announce itself." The term referred to a [research assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_assistant "Research assistant") Greg Pugh who carried around a [rubber duck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_duck "Rubber duck") for this purpose. Variations of the practice use other objects or even pets; [teddy bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teddy_bears "Teddy bears") are especially common.[[2]](#cite_note-2)[[3]](#cite_note-3)

Approach
--------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Rubber_duck_debugging&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Approach")]

Programmers often discover solutions while explaining a problem to someone else, even to people with no programming knowledge. Describing the code, and comparing to what it actually does, exposes inconsistencies.[[4]](#cite_note-cardboarddog-4) Explaining a subject also forces the programmer to look at it from new perspectives and [can provide a deeper understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_by_teaching "Learning by teaching").[[5]](#cite_note-5)

The programmer explaining their solution to an inanimate object (such as a rubber duck) has the advantage of not requiring another human, but also works better than thinking aloud without an audience.[[6]](#cite_note-6) This approach has been taught in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science "Computer science") and software engineering courses.[[7]](#cite_note-7)[[8]](#cite_note-8)

In popular culture
------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Rubber_duck_debugging&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: In popular culture")]

On 1 April 2018, [Stack Overflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_Overflow "Stack Overflow") launched an [April Fools' Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Fools'_Day "April Fools' Day") joke called *Quack Overflow.* A rubber duck avatar appeared in the bottom right corner of the screen, listened to user problems, and pretended to type solutions, only to respond with a simple "quack" sound.[[9]](#cite_note-9) It referenced *rubber ducking* as a powerful method for solving problems.[[10]](#cite_note-10)
