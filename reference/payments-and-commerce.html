Definition — what "taking payment" actually is

You never move money. You move instructions to move money. Every payment is a message between institutions — your business, a payment service provider (PSP), card networks, banks — and the money itself arrives days later, if the message survives. Everything surprising about payments is a consequence of this fact: the settlement lag, the reversal rules, the fees, the fraud surface, and the regulation.

Three terms you must be able to say without flinching:

  • Authorization — asking "is this card good for this amount?" A hold on the card, not money moving.
  • Capture — the moment the money is actually requested. Between auth and capture, the hold can be voided.
  • Settlement — when the money actually lands in your account. Days after capture, in practice.

Every payment is a promise until settlement. The customer's card is good (authorized), the money is requested (captured) — and then the payment can still fail, be disputed, or be clawed back. The merchant's account balance is the last thing to change in the whole chain, and the only fact you can safely trust at the end.

The money-movement parties:

PartyRoleWhose side they're on
You (the merchant)Provide goods/service; request paymentKeep the revenue
The customerAuthorizes payment with their account/cardKeep their money (or get their stuff)
Issuing bankHolds the customer's account, issues the cardThe customer's money is their liability
Acquiring bankHolds your merchant account, receives settlementYour revenue is their liability — they eat chargeback losses first

Why a PSP exists: the alternative is a merchant account with an acquirer, a separate payment gateway, card-network certifications, PCI scope, and a bank relationship each — a full department. A PSP compresses the entire institution stack into an API. The fee you pay is the price of not building a bank.

Commerce vs. payments: payments are the money movement; commerce is the business around it — catalog, cart, orders, inventory, tax, subscriptions, marketplaces. Stripe processes; Shopify (or WooCommerce, or your own stack) sells. The docs' rule: choose the commerce platform by who edits the catalog and who owns the customers; choose the payments provider by geography, fee structure, and developer experience.

Underlying theory — the invariants of money in motion

Five invariants explain almost every payment behavior you'll ever be surprised by:

  1. Money is data until it settles. The only ground truth is the ledger state at a bank, reached days after the click. Everything before that is a promise.
  2. Every leg is a message between institutions, and every message can fail. Authorization can time out; capture can be refused after auth; settlement can be reversed. Your code must survive a failure at any leg.
  3. Idempotency is the contract. Retries are how distributed systems survive, and retries must not double-charge. A charge with the same idempotency key is the same charge, forever.
  4. The webhook is the source of truth. The synchronous API response tells you the request was accepted; the webhook tells you what actually happened. Reconciliation = "did our records match what the provider settled?"
  5. Fraud follows value. Any flow that moves money will be attacked at its softest point — refunds, chargebacks, account takeover, social engineering. Every money-handling feature is a security feature.

Mental models — frames for thinking about payments

  • The pipeline of handoffs. Money flow = merchant → PSP → network → issuer, each leg with its own rules and failure modes. Debug by asking "which leg is this failure from?" — the answer tells you who owns the fix.
  • The promise until settlement. A payment is "successful" only when it settles; treat captures and webhooks as provisional. Your accounting and your optimism both depend on this.
  • The PSP as your bank-shaped dependency. Like a plugin or a database, it's a dependency with an SLA, a fee structure, and a risk policy. It can freeze accounts; it can fail; it has a support queue. Plan for the PSP as a system, not a black box with a nice dashboard.
  • The chargeback as gravity. Disputes are not exceptional — they are the default way money returns in card systems. Design for the reversal before you design for the sale.
  • The three-way fee split. Every card transaction pays interchange (set by networks, most of the cost) + scheme fees + processor markup. Your "2.9% + 30¢" is mostly not yours to negotiate — and some payment methods (ACH, local schemes, wallets) are dramatically cheaper. Fees are a design input, not a cost of doing business.
  • The checkout as a security boundary. The page where card data is entered is the seam between the merchant's app and the PSP's PCI-scoped surface. Whoever hosts that page owns the compliance — which is why hosted checkout is the lazy winner.

How payments work — the mechanics

The card flow, end to end

Customer → card data → PSP → ① AUTHORIZATION request → card network → issuing bank
                                          │
   your site  ←  "approved"  ←  PSP  ←  network  ←  "funds held" (auth hold)
   (you don't see card data if hosted checkout)
                                          │
   ① CAPTURE (you ask for the money, or the PSP auto-captures)
                                          ▼
   settlement in days  →  money in your account  →  reconciliation against webhooks
  • Auth + capture as separate steps matters for order flows: you can authorize at checkout, ship later, capture on fulfillment, and void un-shipped orders. Most simple integrations auto-capture immediately — fine — but know which one you chose, because "we charged them but never shipped" is an auth/capture misconfiguration story.
  • Tokenization: the card number is exchanged once for a token (a merchant-scoped reference), and only the token touches your system. Your database never stores PANs; your compliance scope shrinks to nothing. This is the single most important security habit in payments.

The selection rule: start with cards + wallets (everyone accepts them), add ACH/SEPA for subscriptions (fees compound on recurring), add BNPL only when ticket size justifies it, and let geography decide the rest (PayPal is still a checkout expectation in some markets; Paystack/Mercado Pago rule their regions; Alipay/WeChat Pay are non-negotiable in China). The PSP's country/payment-method coverage is a first-class requirement, not a detail.

Fees — read the three-way split

Your PSP's pricing is "interchange + scheme fees + PSP markup." Interchange is set by the networks per card type/country and is not negotiable by you; the PSP markup is what you actually shop for. Fee levers you control: payment method (ACH beats cards), card present vs. card not present (lower in-person rates), regional processing, and volume negotiations. Treat fees as a product decision — a subscription at 2.9% per month over years is a tax on your margin that ACH avoids.

Multi-currency and international payments — money without borders, rules with borders

A global product meets currency conversion, cross-border fees, and local payment habits on day one. Three rule-groups:

  • How money is stored: integer minor units with an ISO 4217 currency code — 100 cents + USD, never 1.00 as a float. Floating-point is forbidden for money: 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3 in binary, and rounding errors in invoices are how you lose the difference at scale. The amount-currency pair is inseparable — a 100 without a currency is a bug.
  • Who sets the rate: conversion happens at a leg your code doesn't control (the PSP, the card network, or the customer's issuing bank, depending on the product). Watch for Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC) — the customer is offered their home currency at checkout at a poor rate; it's usually a bad deal for them and a fee source for the processor. And know your settlement currency: Stripe settles your whole balance in one currency, so multi-currency sales mean conversion at payout, with FX cost you price into the business.
  • Local methods are the requirement: cards are a minority payment method in much of the world. The PSP's country/payment-method coverage decides who can pay, before your dashboard decides anything.

Integration patterns — where the code meets the money

Hosted checkout — the lazy default

  • Stripe Checkout / Payment Links, Shopify, PayPal, Lemon Squeezy — the provider hosts the payment page; you redirect, they return you (and the customer) to your success page. Zero card data on your servers, zero PCI scope, built-in wallets and 3DS, refunds and receipts managed in a dashboard.
  • When it wins: almost always — small products, client sites, anything where checkout isn't a competitive differentiator. The CMS and freelance paths both default here.

Embedded elements — the custom surface

  • Stripe Elements / Payment Elements, Adyen Components — the provider's SDK renders the card inputs inside your page; you style the UI, the provider still owns the data. Card data touches your DOM but not your servers. Moderate PCI scope (SAQ A-EP-ish via the provider's iframe/JS), full design control.
  • When it wins: checkout design matters (conversion is a business metric), custom pricing at checkout, one-page apps that can't redirect.

API-direct charges

  • POST /v1/payment_intents with your own tokenized payment method — the full control surface: custom flows, saved cards for subscriptions, split payments, everything. You own the entire flow, the error handling, and the compliance scope for whatever data you touch.
  • When it wins: subscriptions, marketplaces, recurring billing, any custom money logic.

Bundled billing (Clerk Billing) — the new lazy default

  • Clerk Billing (launched 2025) bundles subscription billing into the auth provider: you define plans/features in the Clerk dashboard, drop in prebuilt pricing/checkout components, and the active plan ships as claims on the session token — feature gating reuses your existing has() auth check, no webhook plumbing, no subscription table. Stripe only processes; Clerk is not a merchant of record.
  • The trade: 0.7% on top of Stripe's fees; plans are defined in Clerk (not synced with Stripe Billing — your plan_status column is the token, not a webhook table); USD-only so far. Worth it when auth is already Clerk (see Next.js) and billing needs are simple; the webhook-based state machine below still applies when you outgrow it.

The decision rule: hosted checkout unless you have a reason (conversion, custom UX, recurring logic). Every layer of custom integration you take on is PCI scope and failure surface you now own — the same "who owns the failure" math as everywhere else on this shelf. The pattern is provider-agnostic: the state machine, webhooks, idempotency, and reconciliation are the same whether the PSP is Stripe, PayPal, Adyen, or a regional leader — only the dashboard, test cards, and event names change.

Idempotency — the double-charge guard

The rule from Backend Engineering §5, applied to money: a retry must not become a second charge.

  • Every charge carries an idempotency_key — a per-operation unique string (an order id, a UUID you generated per attempt). The provider promises: same key → same charge, forever. Retry-safe by construction.
  • Generate the key per logical operation, not per HTTP request — a client that retries the checkout request must reuse the key, or it's a double charge with extra steps.
  • Your own API should expose the same contract: the client sends an idempotency key, your handler keys the charge on it. (This is the CRUD Create rule from Backend Engineering, and it's how payment APIs stay sane.)

Webhooks — the source of truth, not the API response

Every payment outcome your code needs to trust — paid, failed, refunded, disputed, subscription renewed — arrives as a webhook event, hours or days after the synchronous request. Three rules:

  • Never reconcile from the synchronous response. It tells you the request was accepted; it cannot tell you about capture failures, disputes, or chargebacks that arrive later. The webhook is the ledger.
  • Verify signatures and dedupe. Verify the webhook signature (the provider's secret-based HMAC) on every event, and dedupe by event id — providers retry on failure, and your handler must be idempotent (the Backend Engineering §3 rule, money edition).
  • Handle it as a state transition, not a notification. The payment lifecycle is a state machine (below). The webhook says "transition to this state"; your database records the transition and the timestamp it happened at the provider, not when your handler ran.

The payment state machine

   created ──→ requires_action (3DS) ──→ processing
       │                                  │
       └────────── failed ────────────────┘
                       │
                 processing ──→ succeeded
                       │            │
                       ▼            ▼
                    (no capture)  captured ──→ settled
                                       │
                          refunded ←───┘
                          disputed ──→ won / lost (chargeback)

Every state has a row in your database, and every transition arrives via webhook or a successful synchronous confirm. "We got a webhook that says failed" is normal; "we saw a success page but no succeeded event" is the classic bug — the success page must be client-side optimism, and the state machine must be the server-side truth. Show the user the good news, but make the database wait for the webhook.

Subscriptions — money that repeats

A subscription is a state machine plus a scheduler: trialing → active → past_due → canceled / paused → ended, with renewal attempts on a cadence. The engineering is in the edges:

  • Dunning: when a renewal fails (expired card, insufficient funds), the payment retries on a schedule (e.g., 3 attempts over days) with increasingly firm customer emails. Without dunning, ~30% of churn is just "card declined, never retried."
  • Proration and plan changes: upgrade/downgrade mid-cycle must compute the credit/debit difference — a rounding minefield; let the billing library (Stripe Billing, Recurly, Chargebee) own it.
  • Usage vs. fixed billing: fixed (same amount monthly) is trivial; usage-based (per-seat, per-API-call) requires metering events and end-of-cycle aggregation — a data pipeline with invoices.
  • The money is recurring; so is the risk: subscription chargebacks happen at the failed-renewal boundary, and cancellation UX is where customer-relations karma is made. Make canceling easy — the lazy rule that also retains more customers than any dark pattern.

The lifecycle decisions the billing engine can't make

The provider owns dunning, proration math, and retries — but these product decisions are yours, and customers feel them immediately:

  • Trial conversion: decide the exact moment the trial ends (exact timestamp vs. end-of-day), and what happens when the card fails at conversion — grace period with a clear "update your card" prompt, not an instant lockout.
  • Cancellation timing: immediate or end-of-period? The customer's expectation, your revenue recognition, and your refund policy all hang on this one line in the pricing page.
  • Pause/hold: if pausing exists, define what happens to the billing cycle and to usage during the pause.
  • Grandfathering: when you change pricing, existing subscribers keep their old price until they change plans — and that promise needs to be communicated, not discovered.
  • Free tier → paid: is the free tier a $0 plan or a separate product? The upgrade prompt is a conversion feature, not an afterthought.

Marketplaces and splits — other people's money

Platforms that pay their sellers (marketplaces, marketplaces-of-services, freelancing platforms) move other people's money — which is a legal category, not just a technical one:

  • The architecture: the platform takes the customer's payment, splits it — seller share (via the PSP's connected-account / application-fee system, e.g. Stripe Connect or Adyen platform payments) and platform fee — and the seller's share settles to the seller's own account.
  • KYC is yours: you are responsible for verifying each seller (the PSP's onboarding flows do this) — "know your customer" applies to sellers and buyers; holding or routing other people's money without the right licensing is a compliance crime, not a technical bug.
  • The anti-pattern: receiving customer money into your account and manually sending payouts ("the PayPal-and-a-spreadsheet marketplace"). That's called "operating a money-transmission business without a license," and it is how small platforms die. Use the PSP's split/settlement rails; they exist exactly for this.
  • Failure modes: seller payouts failing (bank details wrong), seller disputes eating platform fees, and the marketplace itself being on the hook for refunds when a seller vanishes — platform liability is a business-model decision, priced into the fee, not a code decision.

Refunds, chargebacks, and disputes — the money that comes back

The rules: a chargeback is not an appeal — you get one evidence window, the bank's bias is toward the cardholder, and repeated disputes can get your processing terminated (high dispute ratios are a PSP-offboarding cause). The professional posture is prevention (clear descriptors on statements, delivery tracking, good support) plus a repeatable evidence workflow, not outrage. Refunds are always cheaper than disputes; the "no refunds ever" policy converts into chargeback fees.

PCI DSS and compliance — the rules you inherited

  • PCI DSS is the card-industry security standard (12 requirements: network security, encryption of card data, access control, monitoring, and more). It applies to anyone who stores, processes, or transmits card data — which is why the entire industry's lazy move is not being in scope.
  • Scope is a design choice. Hosted checkout → SAQ A (the lightest, "we don't touch card data"). Iframe/JS elements → SAQ A-EP. Storing PANs yourself → full SAQ D audits, a serious program. The tokenization habit (and the security discipline behind it) is how the scope stays small.
  • What actually matters in practice: never store full PANs, log full card numbers, or print them; use the provider's tokenization everywhere; keep secrets out of code; and let the PSP's dashboard/JS do the heavy lifting. Compliance is mostly "don't have card data" plus "patch your stuff" — the same hygiene as the rest of this shelf, with a named standard and fines attached.

Fraud — the tax on trust

Every money flow is attacked at its softest point. The fraud surface, in practice:

AttackHow it worksDefense

The tradeoff is real: every fraud control adds checkout friction, and friction costs conversions. The professional position is measured: start with the provider's default rules (Radar, Adyen risk, PSP-level 3DS) + CVV + a sanity amount threshold, add velocity rules when the chargeback ratio says so, and price fraud into the business (chargebacks are a cost line, not a surprise). Fraud prevention that breaks your own checkout is a fraud on your conversion.

3D Secure (3DS) — the authentication tax

3DS is the protocol where the customer authenticates with their issuing bank during checkout — a challenge (OTP, biometric, app confirmation) or a silent approval (the frictionless flow). PSD2/SCA mandates it for most EU card transactions, which is why it's the state machine's requires_action state. The provider's risk engine decides when to request it; you can also request it explicitly for high-risk transactions.

The liability shift is the point: without 3DS, fraud chargebacks are your problem; with 3DS, the issuing bank is liable in most cases. That is the entire reason 3DS exists — it trades a little checkout friction for a transfer of fraud risk. The provider balances the tradeoff with its risk engine; your job is only to render the requires_action state honestly (hosted checkout does this for free).

Worked example — a small SaaS takes its first payment

The product: a subscription tool. The constraints: two-person team, no compliance ambitions, must ship this week. The answer to every question is "the laziest correct thing":

Step 1 — the platform decision. Stripe (or your regional equivalent) with Stripe Checkout, a subscription product in Stripe Billing, and the backend receiving webhooks. No card data ever touches the app; PCI scope is SAQ A; dunning, 3DS, and receipts come free. If the checkout page must match the brand later, swap to Payment Elements — but not today.

Step 2 — the order of implementation (the lazy path):

  • Create the subscription product + price in the dashboard. Use the test-mode cards (4242…, declined cards, 3DS cards) — test mode is where you learn every failure before a customer pays you.
  • Redirect the customer to Checkout from your pricing page; success_url and cancel_url handled with client_reference_id = your user id so the webhook can join the dots.
  • Write the webhook endpoint: verify the signature, dedupe by event id, and handle the events that matter — checkout.session.completed (mark the user active), customer.subscription.updated (plan changes, cancellations), customer.subscription.deleted (end), invoice.payment_failed (dunning state). Every handler is a state transition on the user's plan_status column, keyed on provider timestamps. Idempotent: reprocessing an event must be a no-op.
  • Gate the app: the paid feature checks plan_status = active server-side (the authorization rule — a client-side flag is marketing, not entitlement).
  • Reconciliation habit from day one: a weekly script (or just a dashboard query) that compares "users we think are active" vs. "subscriptions the provider thinks are active". Mismatches are how silent revenue leaks are found.

Step 3 — the first crisis, handled calmly. A customer's card fails at renewal. The webhook says past_due; Stripe's dunning retries; the customer gets the provider's email; the product shows "payment failed — update your card" via your own email triggered by the webhook. You did not write a retry loop, a card-update flow, or a dunning scheduler — the provider did, and your job was only to render the state honestly. That's the whole lesson of this doc: know which layer is yours. The state machine is yours to store and render; the banking is the provider's; the compliance is whoever touches card data; the customer relationship is everyone's.

Test the webhook path explicitly — it's where production bugs live:

  • Verify signatures work (send an unsigned event; it must be rejected).
  • Send the same event twice — the handler must be idempotent (no second activation, no double credit).
  • Send events out of order (a succeeded before its processing) — the state machine must tolerate it.
  • Simulate a delivery failure — the provider retries; your handler must survive a replay.

For subscriptions, use test clocks (Stripe test clocks) to fast-forward time: trial → paid conversion, failed renewal → dunning → cancellation, and proration on a mid-cycle plan change. The full lifecycle, on demand, in minutes.

Reconciliation — proving the numbers

Reconciliation is the question "did our records match what the provider settled?" — concretely:

  1. Pull the provider's settlement report (or query the API for the period's payments, refunds, chargebacks, and payouts).
  2. Compare against your own payment records: every payment you think succeeded, every refund, every dispute, every payout amount (including fees).
  3. Flag mismatches. App says succeeded, provider says failed → a missed webhook; update your state from the provider's record. Amount mismatch → FX conversion, fees, or a partial refund. Missing payment → webhook delivery failure.
  4. Resolve: most mismatches are missed webhooks, and the fix is "update your state from the provider's record" — not a manual ledger edit.

The lazy rule: automate the comparison, escalate the mismatches. A weekly scheduled job beats a human dashboard-scroller, and the audit trail is a product, not a chore. Frequency: daily for high-volume products, weekly otherwise, after every payout for marketplaces.

Merchant of record (MoR) — Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, FastSpring, Polar sit one level above a PSP: they are the legal seller of your product. They own sales tax/VAT calculation and remittance, refunds, chargebacks, and PCI scope — the entire compliance surface — for a larger fee (typically 5%+ vs. ~2.9% processing). The tradeoff: the MoR's name appears on the customer's bank statement, checkout customization is limited, and the fee compounds on volume. Worth it when you sell globally and the fee premium is cheaper than a tax/compliance person; not worth it when volume is high or the checkout is a brand surface. Note: Stripe acquired Lemon Squeezy (2024), which continues standalone; Polar is the open-source indie option.

The future — where money movement is going

SignalWhat it changes
Account-to-account / open bankingInstant bank transfers with no interchange and no chargebacks — the fee structure and the risk model both shift; PSD2-led in Europe, spreading
Instant settlementReal-time rails (Faster Payments, FedNow, SEPA Instant) compress the settlement lag — the "promise until settlement" window shrinks toward zero
Embedded financePayments, lending, and banking become APIs inside every product — the PSP stops being a checkout and becomes a business stack
Regulation tighteningPSD3, DORA, consumer-protection rules — compliance becomes a product feature; merchant-of-record models grow
Stablecoins & crypto railsBorderless settlement with new custody/regulation risk — real in specific corridors (cross-border payouts), hype everywhere else

The direction: faster settlement, lower fees, more abstraction, more regulation. The invariants hold — money is data until it settles, every leg can fail, retries must be idempotent — but the legs get faster and the regulators get closer. The skill is unchanged: know exactly where your code's responsibility ends and the institution's begins.
