Definition — what "open source" is, and what it is not

Open source is a practice of licensing and collaboration: source code made publicly available under a license that grants others the right to view, modify, and distribute it. That last word — license — is the whole legal engine. "Open source" is not "free," not "no license," and not "public domain":

TermWhat it meansThe trap
Open sourceSource available under a license granting use/modify/distributeSome "open" licenses restrict commercial use — read the license, not the label
No licenseDefault: all rights reserved by the authorSource on GitHub with no license is not usable — you cannot legally copy it
Public domainAll rights waived (CC0, Unlicense)Rarely automatic; waiving rights legally is itself a license choice
Free softwareThe GNU four freedoms: run, study, share, modifyIdeologically stricter; nearly all free software is also open source (the reverse is not always true)
Free of chargePrice = 0Unrelated to either; MIT code is often sold inside commercial products

The practical rule: a project's LICENSE file is the contract that makes it usable by you, and that governs what you may do with a contribution. Read it before you depend on the project, and know it before you contribute.

History — how the commons came to be

The short version: the 1980s saw proprietary software lock down source code; Richard Stallman founded GNU and invented copyleft to guarantee the right to share; Linus Torvalds released Linux under the GPL in 1991; Eric Raymond's 1997 essay "The Cathedral and the Bazaar" named the peer-production model; and in 1998 the term "open source" was coined as a pragmatic, business-friendly rebrand of the same practice. The shelf's history timeline in Computer Science & Software Engineering covers this in depth — this doc inherits that history and focuses on the present practice.

Underlying theory — the commons, and how it stays alive

Open source works because of a specific economic shape: the cost of copying is near zero, and the value of a shared pool grows with contributors. Three mechanisms keep the pool healthy:

  • Linus's Law. "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow." Code is reviewed in public; the review itself is the quality mechanism. This is why your contribution's reviewability matters as much as its correctness.
  • Scratch an itch. Most contributions are people fixing their own problem, not charity. The best first contributions are the ones you personally need — the "good first issue" framing is how projects lower the barrier to that.
  • The maintainer bottleneck. Contributors are many, maintainers are few, and maintenance is unpaid. The scarcest resource in open source is not code — it is review attention. Every contribution either spends or earns that attention.

Licenses — the legal contract, permissively or copyleft

Licenses fall into two families. The difference is what happens to derivative work:

FamilyCommon licensesThe ruleFor you as a contributorFor you as a consumer
PermissiveMIT, BSD, ISC, Apache-2.0Anyone can use, modify, and distribute, including in closed/commercial softwareYour contribution is licensed to the project under the same terms; no obligations follow youUse it in anything; only constraints are attribution (MIT/BSD) and patent clauses (Apache)
Copyleft (strong)GPL-3.0Derivative works must also be GPL — the source stays open foreverYour code goes into a permanently-open poolIf you distribute a modified version, you must publish its source under GPL
Copyleft (weak)LGPL, MPL-2.0Library can be linked into proprietary software; changes to the library itself stay copyleftSame as GPL for the library's own codeYou may link it from closed code without open-sourcing yours
Network copyleftAGPL-3.0GPL, extended to software served over a network (a SAAS loophole closer)Same as GPLIf you serve a modified AGPL app to users, you must offer its source

Choosing a license for your own project: if you want maximum adoption, MIT. If you want to guarantee your code stays open forever, GPL. If you don't want to choose, choosealicense.com walks the decision. And one practical note: a project's license usually requires contributors to license their code under the project's terms — some projects ask you to confirm this with a Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO, "Signed-off-by") or a Contributor License Agreement (CLA) before merging your PR.

Compatibility — which licenses can combine. The rule that governs every dependency: permissive licenses (MIT, BSD, Apache) combine with anything, including proprietary code. Copyleft licenses do not — GPL requires any work that links or incorporates it to also be GPL, which is why proprietary products avoid GPL dependencies and SaaS avoids AGPL. Weak copyleft (LGPL, MPL) lets closed code link the library without open-sourcing the caller. The practical read for a consumer: read the LICENSE before you depend on it — if you're building proprietary software, a GPL dependency forces your whole product open.

Who owns your contribution. By default you own code you wrote; contributing it under a project's license is a grant, not a transfer. The exception is code written as part of your job — your employment contract often assigns that copyright to your employer, so check before contributing "I wrote it at work" code. The DCO/CLA you sign is the project recording this ownership line, not creating it.

Governance — who runs the project, and how

Governance decides who can merge, who decides scope, and how disputes resolve. It shapes your experience as a contributor more than you'd think:

ModelExamplesHow it feels to contribute
BDFL (one benevolent dictator)Python (van Rossum), early ReactFast decisions, strong taste; the single maintainer's view dominates
Meritocracy (trust earned by work)Apache, the Linux kernelGradual: start with small fixes, earn commit access over many contributions
Foundation-governedCNCF, OpenJS, the React Foundation (2026)Formal structure, legal backstop, slower but more stable; contribution paths are documented
Corporate-backedVercel/Next.js, Meta/React, Google/AngularEmployees dominate maintainership; your contribution may need a sponsor on the team to merge

The practical read: a project's governance model is visible in its CONTRIBUTING.md and its maintainer list. Foundation and meritocratic projects reward patient, incremental contribution; corporate-backed ones often need an issue first, and sometimes an internal champion. Read the room before you commit effort.

The contributor journey — from finding a project to your first merge

The path is the same for every project. The mechanics (fork, branch, PR, merge) live in Version Control, CI/CD; here is the strategy of the journey:

  1. Find the project. The best targets are ones you already use — a package in your lockfile, the CLI you run daily. Searching for "good first issue" on GitHub surfaces the intentionally-labeled entry points. Read the project's scope first: its README, its issues, and what's deliberately out of scope.
  2. Read the conventions before writing code. CONTRIBUTING.md names the workflow; the lint and test setup names the standards; the commit style is visible in git log. Your PR is judged against all three. A project's review culture is one of the most important factors to consider before committing your time — if a project has no maintainer activity for months, a PR is a wasted gift.
  3. Claim the issue. Say "I'd like to work on this" on the issue before you start — it prevents two people doing the same work, and it starts a conversation with the maintainer.
  4. Make the smallest useful change. A focused PR that fixes one thing well beats a broad PR that touches ten. The scope rule is the same one from Product Strategy — small slices merge, big ones stall.
  5. Write a PR that reviews itself. Explain what you changed, why, and how you tested it. Link the issue. The easier you make the maintainer's job, the faster it merges.
  6. Survive review. Feedback is about the code, not you. Respond to every point; where you disagree, push back with evidence — respectful pushback is normal and respected. Expect a few rounds.
  7. Merge, then repeat. The first merge is the emotional milestone; regular contribution is the career signal. Maintainers notice contributors who show up, take feedback, and keep going.

How to choose between candidates — the health signals. Step 1 says "find a project you use," but between two projects you use, pick the one that will actually review your PR. Five minutes on the repo answers it:

SignalHealthy meansWhere to look
Commit activityCommits within the last ~30 daysRepo "Commits" page
Issue responseIssues answered within about a weekRecent issue threads
good first issue labelMaintainer explicitly welcomes newcomersGitHub issue search
CONTRIBUTING.mdExists and names the workflowRepo root or .github/
CI statusGreen — the quality gate worksCI badge / Actions tab
Contributor countMore than a couple of active peopleRepo "Contributors" page

The red flags are the same signals inverted: no commits in months, issues left unanswered, a red CI, or a hostile tone in threads — all mean a PR is a wasted gift. And match your skill to the need: docs, tests, bug fixes, translation, and design are all valid first contributions, not just features. A "good first issue" you can fix in an evening beats an important one you'll still be wrestling in a month.

Filing an issue is a contribution too — a well-written bug report is the fastest way to start being useful before your first PR. The minimum: a title that names what and where ("Crash on empty config in load()"), the exact reproduction steps, what you expected vs. what happened, and your environment (OS, versions). The highest-value addition is a minimal reproduction — the smallest code that triggers the bug — because it lets the maintainer debug without your setup. Vague beats nothing, but "The app crashes" is a burden; "This 10-line snippet crashes when handleClick gets a null" is a gift. For a feature request, say what problem you're solving and how people work around it today — not the API you assume — and maintainers read it as product thinking, not a demand.

The emotional arc is real and normal: imposter syndrome before the first PR, anxiety during review, relief at the merge, then — after enough contributions — being offered review duties or maintainer access. That arc is the whole point; it is how the community renews itself.

Code review — from both sides of the table

As a contributorAs a reviewer
Read feedback as a description of the code, not of youComment on the code, never the person; say what and why, not who
Ask when a comment is unclear — clarification is freeOffer suggestions as suggestions, not commands — "how about" over "do this"
Push back when the code is right and the reviewer is wrong — with evidence and graceDistinguish blocking issues (correctness, tests, scope) from nice-to-haves
Rebase and keep the branch tidy rather than stacking commitsReview in small pieces; a 30-line PR gets far more attention than a 500-line one
Say thanks when a reviewer spends their scarce attention on your workRemember review is unpaid — gratitude is the payment that keeps reviewers reviewing

Review is the quality mechanism Linus's Law describes. The fastest way to become a better contributor is to review other people's PRs first — reading someone else's code against the project's conventions teaches you the standards you'll be held to.

Maintaining a project — the other side of the wall

Contributing and maintaining are different crafts. If you maintain:

  • Triage is the job. Issues and PRs accumulate faster than you can act. Close duplicates, mark stale items, label intent — a well-triaged tracker is a calm tracker.
  • Releases are a discipline, not an event. SemVer, changelogs, tags, and CI publishing — the release mechanics live in Version Control, CI/CD §5b; the habit is yours to build. Release small and often; the community trusts a project that ships.
  • Burnout is the systemic risk. Maintainership is unpaid, endless, and public. Sustainable projects automate (CI, templates, bots), document (CONTRIBUTING, ROADMAP), and delegate (trusted contributors, triage teams).
  • Handing off is a success, not a failure. Archiving, transferring to a foundation, or naming new maintainers keeps the commons alive when your life changes. A graceful handoff is how mature projects survive their founders.

Business models — how open source pays for itself

Open source code is free; the work around it is not. The models that keep projects alive (each has a licensing consequence worth knowing):

ModelHow it worksExampleLicense note
Open coreFree core, paid enterprise featuresGitLab, GrafanaCore is usually permissive or copyleft; paid parts stay proprietary
Hosted / SaaSFree software, paid hostingSupabase, PlanetScaleAGPL is chosen precisely to stop competitors re-hosting the same code
Support & consultingFree software, paid expertiseRed Hat (historically)Works with any license; the value is the people
Sponsorship / donationsVoluntary funding of maintainersGitHub Sponsors, Open CollectiveIndependent of license; sustains the people, not the product
Dual licensingFree under one license, paid for a different oneMySQL, MongoDBThe paid license grants terms the free one deliberately doesn't

The income dimension — how much, and whether it's a realistic primary income — is covered in Frontend Income Markets §7. Here, the takeaway is the licensing consequence: a project's funding model and its license are chosen together, and the license you pick for your own project shapes what funding is possible.

Contributing to AI-agent CLIs — the concrete case

The most direct place to apply all of this is the CLI tools you use daily. AI-agent CLIs (Claude Code, Codex, Aider, Continue) are open source projects with unusually clean extension points, and they are actively looking for contributors:

  • Where the extension points are. Most expose three seams: tools (what the agent can do), providers (which model/backend it talks to), and prompts/system (how it behaves). A new tool or provider is a classic "good first issue" shape.
  • How to read the codebase. The architecture — REPL loop, tool calling, streaming responses, permission systems — is covered in Building CLIs & Terminal Applications. Read that first; then the repo is familiar territory.
  • The testing challenge is different. Agent behavior is non-deterministic — the same prompt may produce different tool calls. Projects handle this with eval suites (behavioral tests) rather than unit assertions. Understand their eval harness before you write tests that can't be evaluated.
  • Governance is young. Several of these projects are corporate-backed or brand-new, so governance is still settling — which makes early contributions land harder and be valued more.

Principles — the shared constants of open source

  • Read the LICENSE before you touch anything. It is the contract that makes the code usable.
  • Read the room before you commit effort. CONTRIBUTING.md, recent activity, and maintainer responsiveness tell you whether a PR is worth writing.
  • Respect review attention. Small, focused, self-explaining PRs are the ones that merge.
  • The project owns its scope. A "no" to a feature is a scope decision, not a personal judgment.
  • Contribution is a journey, not an event. The first PR is the door; the career signal is the pattern of showing up.
  • Give back what you take. Reporting a bug well, writing documentation, or reviewing one PR is a real contribution — not everything is code.

Worked example — one contribution, end to end

Suppose you use a small open-source CLI for formatting and hit a bug: it mangles files with an unusual path. Here's the full journey:

1 · Confirm the bug: read the README, reproduce in your terminal, search
     the issues (maybe it's already filed — then you comment, not re-file).

2 · Read the room: CONTRIBUTING.md says "fork, branch, PR, add tests."
     git log shows recent activity — the project is alive.

3 · Claim the issue: "I'd like to work on this" + your reproduction.

4 · Read the codebase: find where paths are handled. The CLI architecture
     doc (terminal-applications) tells you where to look — entry → routing
     → the function that processes paths.

5 · Make the smallest fix + a test that fails on the old code and passes
     on the new. Run the project's lint and test suite locally.

6 · Open the PR: one paragraph — what, why, how tested, "Fixes #123".
     The CI runs; it passes.

7 · Review: maintainer asks for a small change ("handle trailing slashes
     too"). You update, push, respond. Second round, merged.

8 · Aftermath: the fix ships in the next release. You filed a bug, fixed
     it, survived review, and the maintainer now knows your name.
     That is the entire career mechanism, compressed.

The same journey on an AI-agent CLI differs only in step 4 (you're tracing the tool-calling seam) and step 5 (tests are eval-based). The arc — find, claim, fix small, survive review, merge — is universal.

The future — trust becomes the product

Two trends are reshaping open source. First, the supply chain became an attack surface — the 2024 xz-utils backdoor showed that a patient contributor can inject a backdoor into a widely-installed library. The response is provenance (SLSA, sigstore signatures, SBOMs) and a sharper eye on who maintains what. Second, AI changes who contributes: models can now draft PRs, which floods maintainers with review work and makes the review bottleneck tighter than ever. The result is the same shift the rest of the shelf predicts — evaluation and judgment become the premium skills. In open source, that means the contributors who understand the code, the project's scope, and the culture — the ones this doc teaches you to be — are more valuable, not less.
