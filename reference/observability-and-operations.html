Reference · The operational layer — knowing the live system is healthy, and finding the fault when it isn't

Observability & Operations

What happens after deploy: choosing the right signals (SLO design), turning them into alerts that respect the humans on call (alert design), running an incident from detection through recovery (incident response), learning without blame (postmortems), and making the knowledge durable (runbooks). System Design owns the architecture of observability; Backend Engineering owns the request-loop instrumentation; this doc owns the operational practice — the discipline that keeps the architecture honest once the code is live.

Language: English · العربية

Definition in one paragraph

Observability & operations is the discipline of knowing whether the live system is healthy, finding the fault when it isn't, and returning it to health without losing what the failure taught you. It owns the judgments that no single signal can make: which measurements deserve a target, which targets deserve an alert, which alerts deserve a page, and which pages deserve a postmortem. It is the layer between "the code shipped" and "the user is fine" — and it is the only discipline on this shelf whose absence is invisible until something breaks.

Definition — what observability & operations is, and what it is not

Operations is the work of running software after it is live: watching it, noticing when it misbehaves, fixing it fast, and making the next failure cheaper. Observability is the property that makes that work possible — the extent to which the system's internal state can be inferred from its external outputs. The two are one discipline because you cannot operate what you cannot observe, and there is no point observing what you will not act on.

Three judgments distinguish this discipline from the docs around it:

JudgmentQuestion it answersOwned here because
What deserves a target? Which measurements are worth committing to? Signal architecture (what to emit) is System Design §6g; choosing which few become commitments is an operational judgment.
What deserves an alert? Which target breaches wake a human? Every alert is a debt against someone's sleep and attention; designing that contract is operational work.
What deserves a postmortem? Which failures earn a written learning? Turning failures into system changes is the loop that makes operations improve instead of repeat.

What it is not: it is not "add logs later" (that is instrumentation), not a dashboard gallery (that is decoration), and not a synonym for monitoring (monitoring is the subset that checks known answers; observability is the ability to ask questions you did not think to ask). The boundary with System Design is precise: System Design designs the system to be understood; operations uses that understanding under pressure, and reports back what the design missed.

History — from paging to telemetry to learning machines

The operational layer has an evolution of its own, and each era left behind the tool category we still use:

EraWhat ran operationsWhat it left behind
Mainframe & telecom (1960s–80s) Operators watched console lights and physical alarms; SNMP (1988) standardized network monitoring. The page — operations as a duty with a human attached, which modern on-call still inherits.
Web 1.0 (1990s–2000s) Nagios (1999) and its descendants: scheduled checks, dashboards, "is the host up?" as the core question. Monitoring as a category — knowing the box is alive, but not why a request was slow.
Log centralization (2000s) Splunk (2003), ELK: free-text logs pulled into one place, searched after the fact. The insight that firehoses of text are not signals — searchable is not the same as understood.
Distributed tracing (2010) Google's Dapper paper: one request's path across many services, end to end. The realization that correlation — not volume — is what localizes a fault in a distributed system.
Metrics 2.0 (2012) Prometheus: pull-based, dimensional, designed for containers; the USE/RED methods followed. Dimensional metrics — labels let one measurement answer many questions (per-service, per-route, per-tenant).
SRE formalized (2016) Google's SRE book: error budgets, SLOs, on-call design, blameless postmortems as published practice. The error budget — reliability as a product decision with a price, not a mood.
OpenTelemetry (2019) OpenTracing + OpenCensus merged into one vendor-neutral standard; W3C trace context on the wire. Interoperable telemetry — traces, metrics, and logs speaking one language across every stack.
The AI era (2023–) LLM calls become spans; agents become systems you must observe; drift replaces crash as the feared failure. An expanding event horizon — see §11.

Evolution — constraint inversions in the operational layer

Same pattern as the rest of the shelf: each era's solution became the next era's constraint.

InversionBeforeAfterWhat changed
Dashboards → SLO burn Watch every metric; alert when any red line crosses. Watch a few commitments; alert when the error budget burns too fast. Alerts stopped enumerating symptoms and started measuring promises. Dashboards became context, not alarms.
Hero on-call → blameless process The one engineer who "knows the system" carries the pager and the memory. Roles, runbooks, postmortems, and shared ownership distribute the knowledge. Failures stopped being personal and started being system properties. The system, not the person, is the unit of learning.
Free-text logs → correlated telemetry Searchable strings; every team invents its own vocabulary. Structured logs + requestIds + trace context; one journey across logs, metrics, and traces. Localization became a property of the platform (System Design §6g), not a skill of the senior engineer.
Uptime as the metric → user impact as the metric "99.9% available" measured by host pings. SLOs measured on the journeys users actually take; availability of a dead endpoint no one calls is free. The definition of "healthy" moved from infrastructure to outcomes.

Underlying theory — what makes operations reliable rather than hopeful

The architecture of the signals (the four signals — metrics, logs, traces, profiles — plus correlation and trace propagation) is System Design §6g's table — this section covers the theory under the practice.

Error-budget arithmetic

  • Availability SLO example: 99.9% over 30 days allows ~43 minutes of error budget. A 10-minute incident burns ~23% of the month's budget — four of those and the budget is nearly gone (~92%).
  • Burn rate: how fast the budget is being consumed relative to the SLO window. 1× = exactly on target; 2× = budget gone in half the window. Burn-rate alerting (multi-window, short + long) catches both fast spikes and slow grinds — the two shapes of budget erosion.
  • The policy consequence: a spent budget is not a shame event; it is a decision trigger — reliability work outranks features until the budget recovers. That inversion is the whole point of having a budget (vocabulary: System Design §6f).

The cognition of production debugging

  • Production debugging is hypothesis testing under time pressure (the method: Problem-Solving §9). The operational discipline exists to shorten the loop: detect → localize → mitigate → verify → learn.
  • Localization without traces is guesswork by availability heuristic — the engineer debugs whatever service they know best, not whatever service failed. This is the Einstellung effect (Problem-Solving §1e) wearing an on-call badge.
  • Cognitive load is the opponent: an on-call engineer juggling the incident, the timeline, the chat, and the dashboard is doing the opposite of deep work. Roles and runbooks exist to move load from the human to the process (underlying theory: cognitive load, Problem-Solving §1e).
The definition that matters: observability (control-theory sense, popularized in software by Charity Majors) is a measure of how well a system's internal states can be inferred from its external outputs. Monitoring asks "is this known thing still true?"; observability asks "what question do I even need to ask?" — the difference between a fire alarm and a sense of smell.

Mental models — how operators actually reason

ModelCore ideaWhen you reach for it
The dashboard is a hypothesis, not a mirror A dashboard is an answer to a question you had yesterday; it shows what you expected to matter. When an incident hides from every existing dashboard — the missing panel is data about your blind spots, not a tooling gap.
An alert is a contract with a human Every page spends a human's attention and health. An alert you cannot act on, or would ignore twice, is a lie you are telling yourself. When reviewing alert rules: delete or fix any alert without a runbook, a dismissal path, or a documented owner.
Reliability is a budget, not a prayer Unreliability is allocated, spent, and accounted for. The question is not "can we be perfect" but "how much imperfection can we afford, and what do we trade for it?" When a feature request races an incident backlog — the budget decides, not the loudest voice.
Drills are scheduled work Restore drills, failover drills, and runbook walkthroughs are maintenance of the process, not optional dress rehearsal. When a runbook has never been executed outside an incident: it is fiction until proven (the drill is the proof).
Every incident ends in the backlog An incident without action items is a story; with action items, it is an investment. The loop closes when learnings return to the product backlog. When a postmortem produces no changes — the meeting was theater (§9).
The system is the unit of learning Blameless does not mean "no accountability." It means the design of incentives, not the character of engineers, is the primary cause to examine. When a "human error" explanation surfaces in a postmortem — ask what the system did to make that error possible.

SLO design — choosing what to promise

Vocabulary (SLI, SLO, SLA, error budget) is defined in System Design §6f. This section owns the practice: which SLIs to pick, what targets to set, and how the budget is governed.

Choosing SLIs — measure the journeys, not the boxes

SLI classTypical definitionDecision metric for choosing it
Availability Share of valid requests answered successfully (e.g., 2xx/3xx, not 5xx) Does a failure here block a user outcome? (A dead admin endpoint nobody calls is free.)
Latency Percentile of request duration over the window (p50, p95, p99) Is the user experience time-sensitive? (p99 catches tail latency; p50 hides it.)
Throughput / capacity Requests or events processed per second, or queue depth Is the system nearing saturation? (Saturation predicts failures before users feel them.)
Durability Share of acknowledged writes that survive (link: RPO/RTO vocabulary in System Design §6f) Does the system promise to keep data? (Silent data loss is the failure no dashboard shows.)
Correctness Share of operations producing the right result (e.g., reconciliation rate, no duplicate charges) Is wrong-but-fast worse than slow? (Payments and inventory live here.)

Setting targets and windows

  • Targets are promises you can keep, not ambitions. If the current p99 is 900ms, a 200ms SLO fails immediately and the budget is a fiction. Start where the product actually needs to be and step down deliberately.
  • Windows hide or reveal slow grinds. A 30-day window absorbs daily spikes; a 1-hour window overreacts to blips. Multi-window burn-rate alerting uses both: a short window to catch fast burns, a long window to catch slow accumulation.
  • Every SLO needs a designated owner — someone who can say "spend the budget" or "feature work stops." An SLO nobody can spend is a poster, not a policy.

Alert design — turning targets into contracts with humans

An alert is a message with a price: someone's attention, sleep, or escalation. The discipline is deciding which target breaches are worth that price, and what the receiver is supposed to do.

The three alert shapes

ShapeFires whenCatchesRisk
Threshold A single metric crosses a fixed value for N minutes Simple, obvious failures (disk full, queue growing) Static numbers drift out of truth; noise from normal variance
Burn-rate (SLO-based) The error budget burns faster than the target over short + long windows Both spikes and slow grinds; the only shape tied to user impact Needs good SLOs to exist; fails silently if targets are fake
Event / anomaly A specific event happens (DLQ receives a message, a poison message appears, a key expires) Known-machinery failures with known fixes (see System Design §6e for DLQ alerting) Duplicate signal from the same root cause; the "alert storm"

The alert design checklist

  • Actionable: the receiver can do something with it. "Rate is high" is a fact; "cache hit ratio dropped below 80% — check the cache-key change from release 4.2 (runbook: cache-storm)" is an action.
  • Directed: page a human for SEV-1/2 only; ticket everything else. Severity vocabulary lives in Software Development Process §9h (SEV-1 = users blocked, SEV-2 = degraded, SEV-3 = minor impact, SEV-4 = cosmetic).
  • Non-redundant: one alert per root cause, not one per symptom. Five dashboards screaming about the same dead database is a storm, not information.
  • Owned: every alert has a human owner and a review date. Alerts are code; they accrue debt and need refactors.
  • Escapable: a documented dismissal path ("known issue, tracked in ticket X") so on-call can silence a known false alarm without guilt — the alternative is teaching people to ignore the pager.

Alert fatigue is a design failure, not a character flaw

When alerts stop being read, the cause is the alert system, not the operators. Fatigue has three sources: too many alerts (each one dilutes the others), alerts without actions (the brain learns they are noise), and alerts without severity (everything pages, so nothing does). The fix is always structural: prune, tie to budgets, add runbooks. The symptom of success: on-call trusts the pager enough to sleep through the ticket-level noise.

Incident response — from detection to recovery

An incident is an unplanned interruption that needs coordinated response. The difference between a fire drill and a fire: roles, a timeline, and a decision-maker.

The lifecycle

PhaseWhat happensWhat it produces
Detection An alert fires, a user reports, a dashboard looks wrong A suspected incident — confirmed by checking user impact, not just the metric
Triage & severity How bad, how fast, how wide? SEV-1 (users blocked) through SEV-4 (cosmetic) Whether to escalate, page, or ticket; whether the incident commander takes over
Command Roles assigned: Incident Commander (decides), Scribe (timeline), Comms (announcements), SMEs (investigate) One decision-maker, one record, one voice — the antidote to ten engineers chasing ten theories
Mitigation Rollback first (fast, known); forward-fix only when rollback is impossible or slower; feature-flag off as a middle path Users restored — before root cause, when the two conflict
Verification Budgets stop burning; the journey is green again; the fix is observed, not assumed Incident declared over with evidence
Follow-up Postmortem (§5); action items enter the backlog; comms close out; runbooks updated The organization learns; the next incident is cheaper

The rules that make it work

  • Mitigation outranks diagnosis. Stopping user pain is the goal; understanding is the follow-up. The Einstellung trap is investigating the interesting bug while the checkout stays broken.
  • The timeline is written during the incident, not after. The scribe records what happened when — the raw material that makes the postmortem honest instead of reconstructed.
  • On-call has authority. The operator can roll back, disable a flag, or stop a deploy without a committee. Authority without process is chaos; process without authority is theater.
  • Freeze during SEV-1/2. Deploys halt while the incident runs; otherwise the incident and the release pipeline fight and both lose.
  • Comms are a deliverable. A status line exists for every incident ("investigating / mitigating / monitoring") — for internal stakeholders and, where user-facing, for customers. Silence reads as chaos, even when it isn't.

Postmortems & learning — making failure an investment

Blameless by design

A blameless postmortem is not "no accountability." It is the discipline of asking what in the system made this failure possible before asking who. Every "human error" explanation is a design question in disguise: the deploy button that lacked a confirmation, the runbook that rotted, the alert that fired at 3am with no action. The system is the unit of learning (§1f).

The postmortem template

SectionWhat it containsWhy it matters
Timeline Detection → escalation → mitigation → recovery, with timestamps (from the scribe) Fixes the record; prevents "we actually responded fine" nostalgia
Impact Users affected, error budget burned, money or reputation cost, duration Converts drama into accounting; the budget is the scoreboard
Root cause & contributing factors The triggering change and the conditions that let it hurt (no load test, no canary, alert too loose) Fixes the soil, not just the weed; contributing factors are where the real changes live
Action items Concrete changes, each with an owner and a ticket; tested/burn-in items marked The only part that improves the system; everything else is narrative
What went well Detection speed, rollback smoothness, comms clarity Protects the practices that worked from being redesigned away

The loop closes in the backlog

Postmortem action items are product work: they compete for the same sprint capacity as features, and the error budget decides the winner (§2). This is the same loop Software Development Process §9h describes ("operational learnings return to the backlog") — this doc's contribution is the craft that makes the learnings real: owners, tickets, and verification instead of vibes.

Runbooks — making the known response boring

A runbook is a written procedure for a known scenario, written before the incident — the difference between "we'll figure it out" and "we already did, here's how." The test of a runbook is not that it exists; it is that it has been executed in a drill (§1f).

The runbook shape

FieldContent
Symptom What the alert or user report looks like (the trigger conditions)
Check The 2–3 commands or dashboards that confirm the scenario (not the whole investigation)
Act The concrete fix or mitigation, with the exact command or flag
Verify How to confirm the fix landed (metrics recover, budget stops burning)
Escalate When to stop following the book and page the owner (never make "keep trying" the last step)

The ownership rule: every runbook has an owner and a review date, because a runbook that describes a deleted service is worse than none — it wastes the exact minutes it claims to save. Alerts link to runbooks; runbooks link to the backup escalation page. The chain is the product.

Principles — the shared constants

PrincipleWhy it is constant
Alerts are tied to user impact Signals exist to protect journeys, not components. A green dashboard beside broken users is the classic failure of monitoring-as-decoration.
Observability ships with the service The architecture (System Design §6g) demands it; the practice enforces it at review time — a service without signals is unmergeable.
The error budget decides, not the loudest voice Reliability work vs. feature work becomes arithmetic instead of argument (the whole point of §2).
Rollback is the default mitigation Known beats clever in an incident. Forward-fixes are for cases where rollback is impossible or slower.
Every incident ends in an action item Without a change, the incident was tuition without a course: paid, not learned.
Practice the failure before it happens Drills convert runbooks from fiction to procedure and expose missing authority before it matters.
Blameless systems, accountable fixes Blame-free inquiry plus owned, tracked, verified action items — one without the other collapses into either witch-hunts or vibes.

On-call design — the rotation is a product, not a duty

Alerting decides when a human is woken; on-call design decides which human, how often, and under what conditions — the structure that keeps the pager sustainable.

AspectWhat to coverDecision metric
Rotation schedules Primary/secondary rotations; one-week vs two-week lengths; who hands off to whom Pages per engineer per week; unacknowledged-page rate
Escalation paths Primary → secondary → manager; time-based escalation (page not acknowledged in N minutes) Time-to-ack; percentage of incidents resolved at the first level
Shadow on-call New operators pair with experienced ones before carrying the pager themselves Time from first shadow shift to a confident solo shift
Load targets A budget for pages (e.g., fewer than 2 pages per engineer per week); over budget → invest in alert quality Pages per week; alert-to-ticket ratio
Handoffs Written handoff at rotation change: open incidents, known issues, upcoming changes, pending action items Could the incoming engineer answer a page alone from the handoff alone?

The rule: the rotation is a designed system, not an inherited chore. On-call without compensation, training, or a load budget is how organizations burn their operators and then blame them for being tired.

Capacity planning & load testing — knowing the ceiling

Deployment operations — the two decisions the toolbox doesn't own

The deployment toolbox — rolling, blue-green, canary, feature flags, rollback and migration limits — is owned by Version Control, CI/CD & Deployment §5 and Software Development Process §9i. Operations adds two decisions around that toolbox:

DecisionWhat to doDecision metric

Budget-based abort Judge the canary against the error budget: if SLO burn exceeds the threshold (§2, §3), abort and roll back — never forward-fix mid-incident (§4, §7) Canary burn vs. the abort threshold; time from abort decision to full revert

Chaos engineering — practicing failure on purpose

Drills (§1f, §6) exercise known procedures; chaos engineering exercises unknown failure by injecting it deliberately, in controlled doses.

PracticeWhat to doDecision metric

Worked example — the checkout incident

One incident, end to end, exercising every section of this doc. The system: a storefront (Gateway → Checkout API → Inventory API → Postgres), with requestIds and OpenTelemetry traces wired per System Design §6g, and a checkout SLO of 99.9% availability / p99 < 300ms per System Design §6f vocabulary.

Detection — 09:41 UTC (Tuesday)

A burn-rate alert fires: checkout error budget burning at 14× over the short window. The alert links to the cache-storm runbook (it does not — yet — describe this exact change, but it names the first checks). One engineer confirms user impact: checkout p99 is 2.1s and error rate is 8%. SEV-2 declared.

Triage & command

The Incident Commander (IC) takes the call in the incident channel; a scribe starts the timeline; an SME pulls the release list — a cache-key change shipped 40 minutes earlier in release 4.2. The IC applies the freeze: no further deploys.

Localization — the correlation loop

The SME opens one failing checkout request by requestId: the trace shows Gateway → Checkout (slow) → Inventory (fast) → Postgres (fast). The hotspot is Checkout's cache layer — a miss storm. The dashboards confirm: cache hit ratio dropped from 92% to 31% at the same minute release 4.2 landed. The new cache-key scheme (which added a timestamp segment to bust stale inventory prices) made every key unique per minute: correct but unreachable — the cache was now a per-minute ornament.

Mitigation & verification

The IC chooses rollback-first: release 4.2 is reverted and the deploy pipeline is gated. Within six minutes the hit ratio recovers to 91% and the budget stops burning; the duration is confirmed on the timeline. The incident closes at 10:12 — 31 minutes from first alert to verified recovery. No data was lost; the damage was latency and eight minutes of errored checkouts.

The postmortem — Thursday

  • Timeline: reconstructed from the scribe's record, including the 8-minute gap between release and first alert (the alert was budget-based, correct — but three minutes of the burn predated detection).
  • Root cause: the cache-key change — correct reasoning, untested behavior under load.
  • Contributing factors: no load test exercised the new key cardinality; no dashboard watched cache hit ratio as a release gate; the cache-storm runbook lacked the "new key scheme" entry (the runbook that existed for the old scheme was followed, then diverged).
  • Action items (each with an owner and a ticket): add key-cardinality check to cache regression tests; add a cache-hit-ratio SLO-watch alert (ticket-level, not page-level); update the cache-storm runbook with the timestamp-key failure signature; schedule the runbook into next month's drill; burn the budget accounting into the reliability board.
  • What went well: rollback-first discipline; the freeze worked; a single trace localized the fault in minutes instead of hours.

The lesson the example exists to teach: no step was heroic. Detection was a budget, localization was correlation, mitigation was a rollback, learning was tickets. That is the discipline — replacing heroics with procedure, and procedure with proof.

The same incident without the discipline — the failure variant

Rerun the story with the practice removed: no SLO burn alert (detection comes from a customer complaint on social media), no trace correlation (the engineer on call debugs the service they know best, not the failing one), no Incident Commander (five engineers chase five theories across three channels), no freeze (a second deploy lands mid-incident and changes the failure's shape), no scribe (the timeline is reconstructed from memory on Thursday), and no postmortem actions (the rollback happened, "someone" did it, and the cache-key change quietly returns in release 4.4). The outcome: hours instead of 31 minutes, two unrelated regressions introduced during investigation, and a team that still cannot say why checkout broke. The discipline is the difference — it is why every section of this doc exists.

The future — where the operational layer is heading

  • LLM calls as spans. A prompt is a request; a completion is a response; a model call is a span in a trace. The correlation loop of §8 extends unchanged: one requestId carries a user's journey through gateway, logic, and model.
  • Agents as systems to observe. The Agent Development Lifecycle (Software Development Process §9d) ends in "deploy with guardrails, monitor for drift." Drift — an agent's behavior diverging from its intended task — replaces the crash as the feared failure mode, and detection needs the same signals: budgets, traces, and alerts that respect humans.
  • eBPF and continuous profiling. Kernel-level instrumentation and always-on sampling shrink the gap between "the system looks fine" and "here is exactly where the CPU went" — more of the unknown made answerable before the question is asked.
  • AI-assisted incident response. Summaries, correlation suggestions, and runbook lookups will compress the investigation loop. The constants do not move: a human owns the decision, the timeline stays honest, and every incident still ends in an action item.

Summary — the operational checklist

  • Can you name the journeys users actually take, and the SLO for each? (If not: §2)
  • Does every alert page a human only when a budget burns, and ticket the rest? (§3)
  • Does every alert have a runbook, an owner, and a dismissal path? (§3, §6)
  • Is the definition of "health" built on user impact, not host pings? (§1c)
  • When the pager fires, does one person decide, one person write the timeline, and one voice speak? (§4)
  • Is rollback the default mitigation, with authority granted to the person holding the pager? (§4, §7)
  • Does every incident produce action items with owners — and do those items enter the backlog like any other work? (§5, §1f)
  • Have the runbooks been executed in a drill this quarter? (§6, §1f)
  • Is the error budget consumed publicly, and does it decide feature-vs-reliability arguments? (§2, §7)
  • Could a brand-new engineer localize a failed request with one requestId and one trace? (§8 — that is the whole point)
  • Can you name your operational maturity level, and the next practice to adopt from it? (If not: §7h)
