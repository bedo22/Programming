1. Definition — what the SDLC is, what a methodology is, what Agile is

The SDLC — the phases

The Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is the sequence of phases a software system goes through from birth to retirement: Requirements → Design → Implementation → Testing → Deployment → Maintenance (Wikipedia). The SDLC describes what happens — the phases, the deliverables, the transitions. It doesn't prescribe how you navigate them.

A methodology — how you navigate the phases

A methodology prescribes how you move through the SDLC (Wikipedia). Waterfall does the phases sequentially (finish Requirements before starting Design). Agile does them iteratively (a little of each phase, every sprint). Spiral does them in risk-driven cycles. A methodology is a blueprint for navigating the SDLC — it tells you the order, the cadence, and the roles.

Agile — an umbrella philosophy, not a single framework

Agile is not a single methodology — it's an umbrella philosophy expressed in the Agile Manifesto (2001). Under the umbrella sit specific frameworks: Scrum (the most widely adopted), Kanban (flow-based), XP (engineering practices), Lean (waste elimination), and DevOps (continuous delivery). They all share the Manifesto's 4 values and 12 principles, but implement them differently (Wikipedia).

What this doc is NOT

  • Not The Rhythm. The Rhythm (L7) is the micro-process — how an individual builds one feature (Model → View → Flow, minutes to hours). This doc is the macro-process — how a team navigates the SDLC (sprints, backlogs, ceremonies, weeks to months). They're at different scales; §7 explains how they connect.
  • Not just project management. Agile includes project management practices (sprints, backlogs, stand-ups), but it also encompasses development practices (TDD, pair programming, CI), team organization (self-organizing, cross-functional), and a philosophy (the 4 values + 12 principles). Project management is one slice, not the whole.
  • Not a framework. Agile is the philosophy; Scrum, Kanban, and XP are the frameworks. Saying "we do Agile" is like saying "we do science" — it tells you the philosophy, not the specific practices.

1b. History — from Waterfall to Agile to AI agents (1970–present)

The software development process has a 55-year lineage, driven by a recurring theme: humans are bad at predicting what software should do, so processes that embrace change beat processes that resist it.

YearEventWhat it contributed
1970Waterfall (Winston Royce)Royce's paper is often cited as the origin of Waterfall — a sequential process: Requirements → Design → Implementation → Testing → Deployment. But Royce himself emphasized feedback between phases and warned that large systems should not be built as a single linear pass. The simplified sequential version later became known as "Waterfall." The myth of "design everything upfront, build once" was born from the diagram, not the paper.
1986Spiral Model (Barry Boehm)One of the first formal loop-based process models. Each spiral cycle: Plan → Risk Analysis → Engineering → Evaluation. Risk-driven: the level of detail depends on the risks to resolve. A major formalization of iterative development. (See also The Rhythm §11.)
1980s–90sIID — Iterative & Incremental DevelopmentLarman & Basili's 2003 IEEE paper traced this back to the 1950s — older than Waterfall. Project Mercury (NASA) was built iteratively. The spiral didn't invent the loop; it named what good devs already did.
1995Scrum (Sutherland & Schwaber)Formalized the iterative process as a lightweight framework: timeboxed sprints, daily stand-ups, product backlog, sprint review, retrospective. Based on empirical process control theory — inspect and adapt (Wikipedia).
1999XP — Extreme Programming (Kent Beck)"Make it work, make it right, make it fast." Introduced TDD, pair programming, continuous integration, refactoring. The engineering practices that make Agile work — not just the ceremonies.
2001Agile Manifesto17 software developers met at Snowbird, Utah, and wrote the 4 values + 12 principles (agilemanifesto.org). Not a framework — a philosophy. It declared: individuals over processes, working software over documentation, customer collaboration over contracts, responding to change over following a plan.
2000sKanban (David Anderson)Adapted the Toyota Production System's just-in-time method for software: visualize the workflow, limit work in progress, pull work as capacity permits. Flow-based, not timeboxed. The alternative to Scrum's sprint model (Wikipedia).
2009DevOpsThe convergence of Development and Operations (first a movement, now a set of practices): continuous integration, continuous deployment, infrastructure as code. The practices that made very frequent, low-risk deployments possible. Automation of the Testing → Deployment → Maintenance phases.
2010sSAFe, LeSS, Spotify ModelScaled Agile frameworks — how to do Agile with 50+ teams. SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework) and LeSS (Large-Scale Scrum) added coordination layers. The Spotify Model (squads, tribes, chapters) popularized team topology — though it's a case study of one company's organization, not a formally prescribed universal framework.
2023–2025AI-augmented SDLCCopilot, ChatGPT, Claude assist each SDLC phase: AI writes code, generates tests, drafts requirements, suggests refactors. The human shifts from writer to evaluator. Not a new lifecycle — the same SDLC with AI accelerating each phase.
2024–presentAgent Development Lifecycle (ADLC)A genuinely new lifecycle for building AI agents — autonomous systems that pursue goals, use tools, take actions. The agent IS the product. New phases: agent design (LLM, tools, memory, orchestration) → build (prompt engineering, tool definitions) → evaluate (goal achievement, hallucination, safety) → deploy with guardrails → monitor for drift (Wikipedia).

The pattern: each methodology was a response to a failure of the previous one. Waterfall failed because requirements change. Spiral responded by making change explicit. Agile responded by embracing change. DevOps responded by automating the deployment bottleneck. AI-augmentation responds to the writing bottleneck. The ADLC responds to the emergence of a new type of software entirely. The SDLC phases don't change; how you navigate them does — and what you're building is starting to change too.

1c. Evolution — constraint inversions across the process

The meta-pattern: each inversion tightens the feedback loop. Waterfall's feedback loop: months (discover the bug after deployment). Spiral's: weeks (discover in the next cycle). Agile's: days (discover in the next stand-up or sprint review). DevOps': minutes (discover in CI). AI-augmented's: seconds (discover from the AI's output). The tighter the loop, the faster you learn — this is the same principle as The Rhythm's hot-reload insight (L15).

1e. Underlying theory — empirical process control and Lean

Empirical process control theory — Scrum's foundation

Scrum is based on empirical process control theory, which distinguishes two types of process:

  • Defined process: the steps are known and repeatable (like a manufacturing assembly line). You can specify the process upfront and expect the same result every time.
  • Empirical process: the steps are uncertain and unpredictable (like software development). You can't define the process upfront; instead, you make it visible, inspect frequently, and adapt. This is the "inspect and adapt" loop at the heart of Scrum.

The theory says: when you can't define the process, don't pretend you can — make it visible and adapt. This is why Scrum has daily stand-ups (inspect), sprint reviews (inspect with stakeholders), and sprint retrospectives (adapt the process itself). Each ceremony is an inspection-and-adaptation point.

Lean manufacturing — Kanban's foundation

Kanban is adapted from the Toyota Production System (late 1940s), the origin of Lean manufacturing (Wikipedia). The core insight: limit work in progress (WIP) to expose bottlenecks and improve flow. When you have too much work in progress, everything is half-done and nothing finishes. When you limit WIP, bottlenecks become visible and you can fix them.

The 7 wastes of Lean (adapted from Toyota's muda): overproduction, waiting, transportation, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects. In software: building features nobody uses (overproduction), waiting for code review (waiting), unnecessary handoffs (transportation), gold-plating (over-processing), stale branches (inventory), context switching (motion), bugs (defects).

How the theories connect: empirical process control says "inspect and adapt" (the loop). Lean says "limit WIP and improve flow" (the constraint). Agile combines both: inspect and adapt at a sustainable pace, with limited WIP so the team isn't overloaded. The Rhythm (L7) inherits both at the feature scale: cycle through threads (inspect and adapt), one feature at a time (limit WIP).

1f. Mental models — adaptive vs predictive, empirical vs defined

DimensionPredictive (Waterfall)Adaptive (Agile)
Assumption about requirementsThey're knowable upfront; changes are expensiveThey'll change; the process should absorb change cheaply
PlanningPlan the whole project, then execute the planPlan the next sprint; replan every sprint based on what you learned
Process typeDefined — the steps are known, repeat themEmpirical — the steps are uncertain, inspect and adapt
Feedback loopMonths (discover the problem after deployment)Days (discover in the next stand-up or sprint review)
Success metricOn time, on budget, per specWorking software that users value; adaptability
Risk managementUpfront: identify all risks, plan for themOngoing: the sprint is a risk-management cycle — the riskiest work goes first
Team organizationSpecialized roles, handoffs between phasesCross-functional, self-organizing, co-located

The shift: Waterfall assumes predictability; Agile assumes uncertainty. Neither is universally right — Waterfall works for well-understood problems with stable requirements (building a bridge, a tax calculation); Agile works for evolving problems with uncertain requirements (a startup product, a user-facing app). The mature practitioner knows which to adopt when — the same judgment as CS & SE's "which parameters matter" (L17).

Pull vs push — Kanban's mental model

A second mental model: push-based (Scrum) vs pull-based (Kanban). In push, work is assigned at sprint planning (pushed to the team). In pull, the team pulls work as capacity permits (no required timeboxes, continuous flow). Push optimizes for cadence and predictability; pull optimizes for flow and utilization. Most teams use a hybrid — "Scrumban" — pulling work within a loose cadence.

2. The SDLC — the six phases

Every methodology — Waterfall, Agile, Spiral, DevOps — navigates the same six phases. The difference is the order, the cadence, and the emphasis. Understanding the phases is the prerequisite for understanding the methodologies.

PhaseWhat happensWhat it produces
1. RequirementsWhat should the software do? Who uses it? What are the constraints? Gathered from stakeholders, users, business needs.User stories, acceptance criteria, a product backlog (in Agile) or a requirements spec (in Waterfall).
2. DesignHow will the software be structured? Architecture, data models, API design, component structure, UI/UX.Architecture diagrams, data models, API contracts, wireframes, component designs.
3. ImplementationWrite the code. Build the features. This is where The Rhythm (L7) operates — Model → View → Flow for each feature.Working code. The feature itself.
4. TestingDoes it work? Unit tests, integration tests, end-to-end tests, user acceptance testing, edge-case checking (Problem-Solving §10).Test results, bug reports, a "definition of done" checklist.
5. DeploymentShip it to users. In DevOps: automated via CI/CD. In Waterfall: a one-time release event.Running software in production. Versioned releases.
6. MaintenanceFix bugs, add features, refactor, monitor. Often the majority of a system's lifetime — the phase that's most underestimated.Patches, updates, new versions, monitoring dashboards, incident reports.

How Waterfall vs Agile navigates these phases: Waterfall does them once, sequentially — finish all Requirements, then all Design, then all Implementation, etc. Agile does them every sprint — a little Requirements (backlog refinement), a little Design (sprint planning), Implementation (the sprint), Testing (during the sprint), Deployment (at sprint review/end), Maintenance (ongoing). The phases are the same; the navigation is different. That's the entire Agile/Waterfall distinction in one sentence.

3. The Agile Manifesto — 4 values, 12 principles (2001)

In February 2001, 17 software developers met at a ski lodge in Snowbird, Utah, and wrote the Manifesto for Agile Software Development (agilemanifesto.org). It's not a framework or a process — it's a statement of values that the frameworks (Scrum, Kanban, XP) implement.

The 4 values

Value (left is valued more)Over (right still has value)
Individuals and interactionsProcesses and tools
Working softwareComprehensive documentation
Customer collaborationContract negotiation
Responding to changeFollowing a plan

The crucial nuance: the right side is not wrong — the Manifesto says "while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more." Processes and tools matter; working software matters more. Plans matter; responding to change matters more. This is not a rejection of planning or documentation — it's a reprioritization.

The 12 principles (summarized)

  1. Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.
  2. Welcome changing requirements, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage.
  3. Deliver working software frequently (weeks rather than months).
  4. Business people and developers must work together daily.
  5. Build projects around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done.
  6. The most efficient and effective method of conveying information is face-to-face conversation.
  7. Working software is the primary measure of progress.
  8. Agile processes promote sustainable development. The sponsors, developers, and users should be able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely.
  9. Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility.
  10. Simplicity — the art of maximizing the amount of work not done — is essential.
  11. The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organizing teams.
  12. At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly.

Principle 12 is the metacognition of Agile — it's Schoenfeld's metacognition (L13) at the team level: inspect your own process, adapt. The sprint retrospective is where principle 12 happens. And principle 10 — "simplicity, the art of maximizing the amount of work not done" — is YAGNI (L16) in manifesto form.

4. Scrum — the most widely adopted Agile framework

Scrum is a lightweight framework that implements the Agile philosophy. It was formalized by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland in 1995, based on empirical process control theory. The Scrum Guide defines it precisely: 3 accountabilities (roles), 5 events (ceremonies), 3 artifacts (Wikipedia).

The 3 accountabilities

AccountabilityResponsibility
Product OwnerMaximizes the value of the product. Owns the product backlog — what gets built, in what order. The bridge between stakeholders and the team. One person, not a committee.
DevelopersCreate the increment each sprint. Cross-functional (frontend, backend, testing, design). Self-managing — they decide how to do the work, not just what.
Scrum MasterEnsures Scrum is understood and enacted. Removes impediments. Facilitates the ceremonies. A servant-leader, not a project manager — they serve the team, they don't direct it.

The 5 events (ceremonies)

EventWhat happensDuration
The SprintThe container for all other events. A fixed-length timebox during which a usable increment is created. Typical length is 2 weeks; the Scrum Guide's upper limit is one month. Teams are typically small — often 10 or fewer people.2 weeks (max 1 month)
Sprint PlanningWhat can be delivered this sprint? How will it be achieved? The team selects backlog items and creates a sprint plan.~4 hours (for a 2-week sprint)
Daily ScrumA daily 15-minute inspection event for the Developers. The three-question format — yesterday, today, blockers — is common, but the team may use any structure that helps inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan. Not a status report — a synchronization and inspection.15 minutes (max)
Sprint ReviewDemonstrate the increment to stakeholders. Get feedback. Inspect the outcome and adapt the backlog. This is Pólya's Phase 4 (Look back) with stakeholders.~2 hours
Sprint RetrospectiveInspect the process itself: what went well? What didn't? What will we change next sprint? This is principle 12 — the team's metacognitive loop.~1.5 hours

The durations are common examples, not fixed rules — the Scrum Guide sets only upper bounds (the sprint is one month max; the Daily Scrum is 15 minutes). Sprint Planning, Review, and Retrospective lengths scale with sprint length and are whatever the team's situation justifies.

The 3 artifacts

ArtifactWhat it is
Product BacklogThe single source of truth for everything that could be added to the product. Ordered by priority (the Product Owner decides). Each item is a user story or a feature with acceptance criteria. Committed to the Product Goal.
Sprint BacklogThe subset of the product backlog selected for this sprint, plus a plan for delivering it. Committed to the Sprint Goal — the single objective the team works toward this sprint.
The IncrementThe usable, potentially shippable product at the end of the sprint. Must meet the Definition of Done — the agreed standard for "potentially releasable." Committed to the Definition of Done.

Commitments, not just artifacts. The modern Scrum Guide gives each artifact an explicit commitment: the Product Backlog is committed to the Product Goal, the Sprint Backlog to the Sprint Goal, and the Increment to the Definition of Done. These give everyone a shared target: not just "what we're building" but "what we're aiming at" and "what 'done' actually means." The Definition of Done, for example, might specify: code merged, tests pass, feature flag configured, documented, accessible, deployed and monitored.

Scrum in one sentence: every 2 weeks, the team picks the highest-priority work from the backlog, builds it using whatever micro-process they choose (The Rhythm), inspects daily, demonstrates the result, and reflects on how to improve — then repeats. The macro-process (Scrum) provides the cadence; the micro-process (The Rhythm) provides the execution within each unit of work.

5. Kanban — flow, not timeboxes

Kanban is the alternative to Scrum's sprint model. Where Scrum uses fixed-length timeboxes (sprints), Kanban uses continuous flow — work is pulled as capacity permits, with no required fixed iterations. It was adapted from the Toyota Production System by David Anderson in the 2000s (Wikipedia).

The 6 core practices

  1. Visualize the workflow. A Kanban board with columns (To Do → In Progress → Code Review → Done). Every work item is a card that moves left to right. You can see the state of the work at a glance.
  2. Limit work in progress (WIP). Each column has a maximum number of items. If "In Progress" has a WIP limit of 3 and there are already 3 items, nobody starts a 4th — they help finish what's in progress first. This is the key practice: it prevents context switching and exposes bottlenecks.
  3. Manage flow. Monitor how fast items move through the board. Identify bottlenecks (where items pile up) and fix them. Optimize for throughput (items completed per unit time), not for utilization (keeping everyone busy).
  4. Make policies explicit. Write down the rules: what counts as "done," when can an item move to the next column, who can pull what. Explicit policies make the process inspectable.
  5. Implement feedback loops. Regular reviews (similar to Scrum's sprint review, but not tied to a sprint cadence). The team inspects the board, identifies improvements, and adapts.
  6. Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally. The team improves the process together, using experiments (try a change, measure the effect, keep or revert). This is empirical process control — inspect and adapt — without the sprint ceremony.

Scrum vs Kanban — the key differences

DimensionScrumKanban
CadenceFixed sprints (2 weeks)Continuous flow (no required timeboxes)
Work modelPush — commit to a batch at sprint planningPull — pull work as capacity permits
RolesProduct Owner, Developers, Scrum MasterNo prescribed roles (use your existing structure)
Key constraintSprint timebox (finish in 2 weeks)WIP limit (don't start more than N items)
Best forProduct development with evolving featuresMaintenance, support, operations, continuous work
Change during workDon't add stories mid-sprint (protect the timebox)Add or reprioritize anytime (flow is flexible)

Scrumban — the hybrid most teams use in practice: loose sprint cadence (2-week rhythm) with Kanban's WIP limits and pull-based work. The ceremonies are lighter; the flow is visible. This is what you'll most likely encounter at work.

6. XP, Lean, DevOps — the rest of the landscape

XP — Extreme Programming (Kent Beck, 1999)

XP focuses on engineering practices — the things that make Agile work, not just the ceremonies. While Scrum tells you how to organize (sprints, roles), XP tells you how to code:

  • Test-Driven Development (TDD) — write the test first, then the code. Red → Green → Refactor. This is Pólya's Phase 4 (Look back) built into every line of code.
  • Pair programming — two developers, one keyboard. Continuous code review, knowledge sharing, fewer bugs.
  • Continuous Integration (CI) — integrate code into the main branch frequently (multiple times per day). Automated tests run on every integration. The tighter the integration loop, the faster you discover problems.
  • Refactoring — continuously improve the code's structure without changing its behavior. This is the "make it right" in "make it work, make it right, make it fast."
  • Simple design — the simplest thing that could possibly work. This is KISS (L16) as a coding practice.

Lean Software Development

Adapted from the Toyota Production System (the same origin as Kanban) by Tom and Mary Poppendieck (2003). The 7 principles:

  1. Eliminate waste — anything that doesn't add value to the customer (overproduction, waiting, unnecessary features, rework).
  2. Amplify learning — short iterations, feedback loops, experiments. This is the iterative principle from The Rhythm (L7) at the project scale.
  3. Decide as late as possible — defer decisions until you have the most information. This is YAGNI (L16) at the project level.
  4. Deliver as fast as possible — short cycles from idea to production. The tighter the delivery loop, the faster the feedback.
  5. Empower the team — let the people doing the work decide how to do it. Self-organization, same as Scrum.
  6. Build integrity in — quality is not a phase (testing at the end); it's built into every step. This is XP's TDD + CI.
  7. See the whole — optimize the whole system, not local parts. Don't optimize one team's velocity at the cost of another team's bottleneck.

DevOps — the automation practices

DevOps (2009) is the convergence of Development and Operations — better described as a culture, movement, or set of practices than a strict methodology. Its goal: automate the Testing → Deployment → Maintenance phases so that very frequent, low-risk deployments become possible. The core practices:

  • Continuous Integration (CI) — every code change is integrated and tested automatically. If the tests fail, the team fixes it immediately.
  • Continuous Deployment (CD) — if CI passes, the code deploys to production automatically. No manual release event.
  • Infrastructure as Code (IaC) — servers, networks, and configuration are defined in code (Terraform, Ansible), not set up manually.
  • Monitoring and observability — dashboards, alerts, logs. If something breaks in production, you know in seconds, not hours.

DevOps is the set of practices that made Agile's "working software" promise real — you can't deliver working software frequently if deployment is a manual, risky, once-a-quarter event. DevOps makes deployment routine.

The nesting in one reading: product strategy decides why; the SDLC/methodology decides how the team organizes work; The Rhythm decides how an individual builds a feature; System Design decides how the pieces fit together; CS & SE decides how solutions are evaluated. Each layer constrains the one below it and is judged by the one above.

The lineage is the same: Boehm's spiral model (1986) named the iterative pattern; XP (1999) operationalized it in code ("make it work, make it right, make it fast"); Agile (2001) popularized it at the sprint scale; The Rhythm (L7) applies it at the feature scale. They're the same pattern at different scales — the spiral made visible at the project level (sprints) and at the feature level (rounds).

8. Principles — the shared constants

Across every methodology, certain principles don't change. These are the constants that survive from Waterfall to Agile to DevOps to AI:

PrincipleWhy it's constant
Iterate, don't waterfallYou can't know everything upfront. The spiral (1986), Agile (2001), and The Rhythm (L7) all prove this. Even Waterfall's creator warned against single-pass.
Inspect and adaptSoftware development is an empirical process, not a defined one. Make it visible (boards, dashboards), inspect frequently (stand-ups, reviews), and adapt (retrospectives). This is the deepest Agile principle — from empirical process control theory.
Working software is the primary measure of progressDocumentation, plans, and designs are useful — but they don't prove the software works. Only running code does. Manifesto principle #7.
Responding to change over following a planPlans are valuable; the ability to change them is more valuable. Manifesto value #4. The Lean principle "decide as late as possible" is the same idea: defer commitments until you have the most information.
Simplicity — maximize work not doneManifesto principle #10. This is YAGNI (L16) at the process level. Don't build features nobody needs; don't add process nobody benefits from.
Sustainable paceManifesto principle #8. Overtime produces short-term output but long-term debt (burnout, bugs, turnover). The process should be repeatable indefinitely.
Self-organizing teams produce the best resultsManifesto principle #11. The people doing the work know best how to do it. Command-and-control (assigning tasks) is less effective than autonomy (the team decides).
Continuous attention to technical excellenceManifesto principle #9. Agile is not an excuse for sloppy code — the opposite. TDD, refactoring, CI (XP's practices) are what make fast iteration possible. Speed comes from quality, not from cutting corners.

9. Worked example — a Scrum sprint end-to-end

Let's trace a single 2-week sprint from start to finish, showing how the SDLC phases and the Agile ceremonies map to each other.

Before the sprint: Backlog Refinement

The Product Owner has a product backlog with ~50 items. The team spends 1 hour refining the top items: writing clear acceptance criteria, estimating complexity (story points), splitting items that are too big. This is the Requirements phase, done incrementally.

Day 1: Sprint Planning (4 hours)

The team selects the top 8 items from the product backlog for this sprint. They create a sprint backlog — the 8 items plus a plan for how to deliver them. This is the Design phase at the sprint level: deciding what to build and how.

Days 1–10: The Sprint (2 weeks of building)

Each developer picks a backlog item and builds it using the micro-process — The Rhythm (L7): sketch the data shape, build the skeleton (Round 1), deepen (Round 2), polish (Round 3). This is the Implementation phase.

Every day at 10am: the Daily Scrum (15 minutes). "Yesterday I finished the cart component. Today I'm starting the checkout form. I'm blocked on the API — the endpoint isn't ready." The team syncs, impediments are surfaced. This is the daily inspection point.

Testing happens during the sprint, not after — unit tests, integration tests, code review. This is the Testing phase, integrated into Implementation (XP's principle: "build integrity in," not "test at the end").

Day 10: Sprint Review (2 hours)

The team demonstrates the working increment to stakeholders: "Here's the cart, here's the checkout form, here's the order confirmation page." Stakeholders give feedback: "The cart looks great, but the checkout form needs a guest checkout option." The product backlog is updated. This is Pólya's Phase 4 (Look back) at the team level — inspect the result, adapt the plan.

Day 10: Sprint Retrospective (1.5 hours)

The team reflects on the process itself: "What went well? The daily scrums kept us synced. What didn't? The API blocker cost us 2 days — next sprint, we should check API readiness in planning. What will we change? Add a 'dependencies' check to sprint planning." This is metacognition at the team level — Manifesto principle #12 in action.

After the sprint: Deploy and repeat

The increment is deployed (in a DevOps setup: automatically via CI/CD). The team starts the next sprint — back to planning, with the updated backlog and the retrospective's improvements. The Maintenance phase runs in parallel: bugs from production are added to the backlog, prioritized by the Product Owner, and fixed in future sprints.

The full cycle in one paragraph: Plan what to build (Requirements + Design) → build it with The Rhythm (Implementation) → test during, not after (Testing) → demonstrate to stakeholders (Review = Phase 4) → reflect on the process (Retrospective = metacognition) → deploy (Deployment) → fix bugs in the next sprint (Maintenance) → repeat. All six SDLC phases, every 2 weeks. That's Agile.

9b. The most common failure modes

Failure modeWhat happensThe fix
Cargo cult AgileThe team does the ceremonies (stand-ups, sprints, retrospectives) but doesn't understand the values. They have the form without the function — stand-ups are status reports, retrospectives produce no changes, sprints are just mini-waterfalls.Read the Manifesto. Ask "why are we doing this ceremony?" If the answer is "because Scrum says so," you're doing cargo cult. Each ceremony exists for a reason — understand it.
Velocity as a metricManagement uses story-point velocity to measure productivity and compare teams. Teams game the system: inflate estimates to look faster. Velocity becomes a target, not a measure.Velocity is for the team's internal planning, never for management comparison. Measure outcomes (working software, user value), not output (story points).
Stand-ups as status meetingsThe daily scrum becomes a report-to-the-manager: "I did X, I'll do Y." No inspection, no impediments raised, no synchronization. The manager treats it as a tracking tool.The daily scrum is for the team, not the manager. It's a synchronization: "are we on track? are there blockers?" If nobody raises impediments, either there are none (rare) or people are afraid to speak up.
Story points as deadlinesStory points (relative complexity estimates) become deadlines: "you estimated 5 points, so this should take 5 days." Points are relative, not temporal — they measure complexity, not time.Points are for relative sizing and sprint planning. They're not commitments, not deadlines, not performance metrics. Use velocity (average points per sprint) for forecasting, not for pressure.

New work goes to the product backlog, not the sprint backlog. Exceptions only for genuine emergencies — and the team decides, not the stakeholder.

Eventually, velocity drops to near zero — the "technical debt crisis."Allocate time for refactoring every sprint. Manifesto principle #9: "continuous attention to technical excellence." Technical debt is a loan — it compounds. Pay it down regularly.
Developer burnoutSustainable pace (principle #8) is ignored. The team sprints at 110% every sprint. After 3 months, developers quit. The process is not repeatable indefinitely.Sustainable pace is not laziness — it's the recognition that software is a marathon, not a sprint (the irony of the term). Pace at 80% so you can surge when needed.
Scrum Master as contributorThe Scrum Master is also a developer, so they can't facilitate objectively. They're too busy coding to remove impediments or coach the team.The Scrum Master accountability needs sufficient capacity to remove impediments and coach — in some organizations that's a full-time role, but it may also be shared or serve multiple teams. The key is that the accountability is actually fulfilled: the SM serves the team and isn't so deep in code that coaching falls by the wayside.
Product Owner role not filledNobody owns the product backlog. Items are vague, priorities shift daily, the team doesn't know what "done" means. The PO role is the most under-filled role in Scrum.The Product Owner is one person, not a committee. They must be available daily, have authority over priorities, and write clear acceptance criteria. A missing PO = a rudderless team.

9d. The future — AI-augmented SDLC and the Agent Development Lifecycle

AI is changing the software development process in two distinct ways. Understanding the difference is critical — they're often confused but they're fundamentally different.

Part 1: AI-augmented SDLC — AI assists the human in building traditional software

This is not a new lifecycle — it's the same SDLC with AI accelerating each phase:

SDLC phaseBefore AIAfter AI (Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude)
RequirementsHuman writes user storiesAI assists with analysis, generates draft stories
DesignHuman designs architectureAI suggests patterns, generates boilerplate
ImplementationHuman writes code (the bottleneck)AI generates code — the bottleneck shifts to evaluation
TestingHuman writes test casesAI generates test cases, identifies edge cases
DeploymentHuman configures CI/CDAI-assisted CI/CD, automated deployment
MaintenanceHuman debugs, refactorsAI assists with debugging, suggests refactors

The key shift: the human's role moves from writer to evaluator. AI can generate code in seconds — but is it correct? Efficient? Maintainable? Did it make the right trade-offs? This is the solution evaluation skill from CS & SE §4–§7 (L17). The evaluation skill becomes the premium skill — it's what AI can't do, because AI doesn't know your context.

The fluency trap intensifies: AI makes it easy to feel you understand a solution without building the mental schema. When AI generates an O(n²) solution and you accept it without analyzing the complexity, you've gained a solution but not the skill. The remedy: always evaluate AI-generated code the same way you'd evaluate your own.

Part 2: Agent Development Lifecycle (ADLC) — building AI agents as a new type of software

This IS a genuinely new lifecycle. The thing being built is different: not a website or an API, but an AI agent — an autonomous system that pursues goals, uses tools, and takes actions (Wikipedia). An agent has: an LLM (the brain), memory, planning logic, tool interfaces, and orchestration. Building one is fundamentally different from building a website.

ADLC phaseWhat happensDiffers from SDLC
1. Agent DesignWhich LLM? What tools? How does it remember? How is it orchestrated?SDLC design = data models + components. ADLC design = capabilities and boundaries — what the agent CAN and MUST NOT do.
2. BuildPrompt engineering, tool definitions, fine-tuning, memory config. The "code" is often natural language + tool schemas.SDLC = writing code. ADLC = configuring LLM behavior through prompts and guardrails.
3. EvaluateDoes the agent achieve its goal? Hallucinate? Use tools correctly? Stay within safety boundaries? Output is probabilistic.SDLC testing = correct output? ADLC = correct behavior across inputs, including adversarial. Closer to evaluating a person.
4. Deploy with guardrailsDeploy with safety boundaries: action limits, cost limits, rate limits, human-in-the-loop checkpoints.SDLC deployment = uptime + performance. ADLC = safety and control — an agent that takes actions needs guardrails.
5. Monitor for driftMonitor for: hallucination, drift (behavior changes), prompt injection, cost overruns, safety violations.SDLC maintenance = fix bugs. ADLC = behavioral integrity — is the agent still doing what it should? A new kind of maintenance.

The key distinction: AI-augmented SDLC is AI helping you build traditional software faster. ADLC is building AI agents as a new type of software. The first accelerates the existing process; the second is a new process for a new type of product.

The ADLC ecosystem (emerging, 2024–present)

  • Frameworks: LangChain (dominant), CrewAI (multi-agent), AutoGPT (autonomous), LlamaIndex.
  • Platforms: OpenAI Assistants API, Anthropic Claude (tool use), Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot Studio.
  • Protocols: MCP (Model Context Protocol), Agent2Agent (inter-agent communication).
  • Agent dev tools: Claude Code, Devin, Cursor, GitHub Copilot — AI-assisted and agent-like development tools (a recursion: agents helping build agents).

The ADLC is too young (2024–present) for a standalone reference — it lacks the 55-year history and stable theory the SDLC has. For now, it's a chapter in the bigger story. In 2–3 years, when patterns consolidate, it may warrant its own reference.

9e. How to choose a process — the practical question

Every methodology is a tool, not a religion. The mature question isn't "which Agile framework should we adopt?" — it's "what are our constraints, and which process fits them?" The table is a starting guide, not a rule:

ContextGood fitWhy
Stable, well-understood requirementsWaterfall or plan-driven hybridLow uncertainty; requirements are unlikely to change. Upfront planning buys certainty.
Regulated environment with mandatory gatesHybrid Waterfall/Agile with compliance gatesNeeds auditability and formal approval points; agile flow still helps within phases.
Product development with uncertain requirementsScrumFrequent inspection, adaptation, and stakeholder feedback — the uncertainty isn't going away.
Support, maintenance, or continuous flowKanbanWork arrives continuously; timeboxes are awkward for interrupt-driven work.
Engineering-heavy product with strong testing cultureXPTDD, CI, pair programming, refactoring — practices that keep code quality high while iterating.
High deployment frequencyDevOps / trunk-based developmentAutomates testing, deployment, and observability so shipping is routine.
Team new to AgileScrum or ScrumbanProvides structure while still allowing flow improvements.

The decision inputs: uncertainty level, requirement stability, regulatory constraints, team maturity, release cadence, and how much support/operations work the team carries. There is no universal best — only the best fit for your constraint set.

9f. Process metrics — how to know the process is working

A process you can't measure is a process you can't improve. Metrics fall into five buckets:

CategoryMetricWhat it tells you
FlowLead timeTime from request to delivery — total cycle.
Cycle timeTime from work started to work completed — how fast work actually moves.
ThroughputNumber of items completed per time period.
WIPHow much work is currently in progress. High WIP = context switching and slower finishes.
Cumulative flowWhether work is piling up in one stage (a bottleneck revealed over time).
DeliveryDeployment frequencyHow often you ship — the DevOps metric that separates Agile on paper from Agile in practice.
Lead time for changesHow long a change takes from commit to production.

MTTRMean time to recover from failure — how fast you heal.
QualityEscaped defectsBugs found after release — the real quality signal, not "we wrote tests."
Test coverage / flaky test rateConfidence in the suite, and how much of that confidence is fake (flaky tests erode trust).
ValueFeature adoption / outcome metricsWhether the shipped work actually produced user value — the thing process exists for.
SustainabilityOvertime, burnout, turnoverWhether the pace is repeatable indefinitely (Manifesto principle #8).

The velocity warning: velocity (story points per sprint) is useful for a team's own planning — forecasting how much it can likely take on next sprint. It is a poor metric for comparing teams or measuring productivity: different teams size differently, and any metric used for evaluation gets gamed.

9g. Quality, security, and compliance — the safety gates

Speed without safety isn't agility — it's recklessness. Modern SDLCs increasingly include "shift-left" security: security checks happen during design and implementation, not only after deployment.

  • Threat modeling — reason about how an attacker could misuse the design, before it's built.
  • Dependency scanning — check third-party libraries for known vulnerabilities (Log4Shell made this first-class).
  • Secret scanning — prevent API keys and credentials from reaching the repository.
  • Static analysis (SAST) and dynamic analysis (DAST) — automated code and runtime security checks in CI.
  • Access control review — who can read, write, and deploy what? Least privilege, reviewed regularly.
  • Audit logging — record who did what, when, for accountability and incident forensics.

In regulated environments (finance, health, government), the SDLC also includes compliance gates: change approval records, evidence collection, privacy review, and sign-off before release. These add ceremony — but they're the constraints the process must serve. Security and compliance belong in the Definition of Done.

9h. Operations and incident management — the SDLC doesn't end at deploy

Deployment is not the end. Production software needs monitoring, alerting, incident response, rollback plans, and support processes — a whole operational layer the SDLC phases barely hint at.

  • Monitoring and observability — metrics, logs, and traces so you know the system is alive and why it behaves as it does.
  • Alerts and on-call — the right alerts (not alert fatigue), with a human accountable when things break.
  • Incident triage and severity levels — classify: is this SEV-1 (down, users blocked) or SEV-4 (cosmetic)? Response escalates with severity.
  • Rollback — every release should be reversible; a safe rollback is a smaller deployment risk than the original change.
  • Hotfix process — a fast, still-reviewed path for production-urgent fixes that bypasses the normal queue without bypassing quality.
  • Postmortems — after an incident, ask "what happened, why, what do we change?" — blameless, focused on the system not the person.
  • Runbooks — documented procedures for known issues, so on-call engineers don't improvise at 3am.
  • SLOs / SLAs — the service-level objectives that define what "healthy" means, and the contracts that hold you to it.

The loop closes when operational learnings return to the backlog: bugs get triaged and prioritized, postmortem actions become backlog items, and monitoring improvements are planned like any other work. Operations is not downstream of the SDLC — it feeds it.

The full treatment of this layer — choosing targets, designing alerts, running incidents, postmortem craft, runbooks — lives in Observability & Operations.

9i. Release strategies and environments

Deployment is not just "ship it." Teams move work through environments and choose how much risk each release carries:

StrategyWhat it isRisk profile
Environment ladderDev → staging → production; each environment approximates the next one closer.Catches issues early, but environments can drift from production.
Feature flagsShip the code but turn it on/off at runtime — decouple deploy from release.Low risk per change; needs flag hygiene (remove flags when the feature ships).
Canary releasesRoute a small % of traffic to the new version, watch metrics, then widen.Contained blast radius; requires good observability and fast decisions.
Blue-greenRun two environments; swap traffic to the new one, keep the old for instant rollback.Very fast rollback; doubles infrastructure cost.
Automated rollbackIf health checks fail after deploy, automatically revert.Fast recovery; needs reliable health checks or you roll back good changes.

The safer the release mechanism, the more frequently the team can ship. This is the DevOps insight at the release level: deployment risk is managed by mechanics (canaries, flags, rollback), not by releasing less often.

9j. Testing strategy — the test pyramid

Testing is not a single phase; it's a strategy with different speeds and scopes:

LayerWhat it coversSpeed / cost
Unit testsSingle functions and modules in isolation.Fast, cheap — the base of the pyramid, the most tests.
Integration testsBoundaries between modules: databases, APIs, services.Slower, catches wiring bugs unit tests can't.
End-to-end testsCritical user journeys through the whole stack.Slow, brittle — a few, for the paths that matter most.
Exploratory / UATHumans probing the real product for behavior automated tests miss.Unpredictable, invaluable.

Watch the flaky test. A test that fails intermittently gets ignored, and an ignored test is worse than no test — it trains the team to skip the suite. A healthy strategy balances speed, coverage, and realism, and pays down flaky and out-of-date tests rather than accumulating them.

9k. Branching and integration strategy

Process and version control are connected. The branching model either supports the delivery cadence or fights it:

  • Trunk-based development — everyone commits to the main line (with short-lived feature branches), integrates multiple times a day. Best fit for CI/CD and frequent release.
  • Git Flow — a develop branch plus release branches and long-lived feature branches. More ceremony; suits versioned releases with long support windows.
  • Short-lived feature branches — isolate work for hours to days, merge back fast. Merge conflicts shrink as branch lifetime shrinks.

The rule: long-lived branches increase merge risk and delay feedback. Choose a branching strategy that supports the team's delivery cadence — don't let the branch model become an unintended waterfall between code and integration.

9l. Estimation and planning — forecasts, not promises

Estimates answer "how big is this?" — and they are forecasts, not commitments. Common practices:

  • Story points — relative complexity, not time. Useful for the team's own capacity planning (velocity).
  • Planning poker / t-shirt sizing — converge the team on a shared estimate by discussion, not by the loudest voice.
  • The uncertainty cone — early estimates are wildly uncertain; uncertainty shrinks as work is understood. Estimate accordingly.
  • "No estimates" — some teams skip estimates entirely and rely on cycle-time forecasting from historical data.

The rule: estimates are for planning and forecasting, not for deadlines or performance metrics. The moment an estimate becomes a target, people pad it — and the estimate stops being useful.

9m. Team topology and organizational constraints (Conway's Law)

Process cannot be separated from organization. Conway's Law: organizations design systems that mirror their communication structure — team boundaries become system boundaries.

  • Platform teams — build shared infrastructure other teams consume; reduce duplication.
  • Enabling teams — help other teams build capability in a domain, then move on.
  • Cognitive load — each team should own work it can actually hold in its head; overloaded teams ship less.
  • Team APIs — the interfaces between teams (owned services, owned domains) are as important as code APIs.

The operational reading: when many teams depend on one team or one service, process friction becomes architectural friction. A good process design considers the shape of the organization, not just the shape of the sprints.

9n. Remote and distributed teams

Face-to-face conversation (Manifesto principle #6) is valuable — but modern teams often work remotely. Distributed teams compensate deliberately:

  • Written documentation — decisions, designs, and context in writing, because hallway conversations don't happen.
  • Async communication — default to asynchronous (issue threads, PR reviews, recorded decisions) so time zones don't block progress.
  • Distributed ceremonies — same meetings, run with the same rigor: cameras on, a written agenda, everyone heard, decisions captured.
  • Intentional collaboration time — scheduled overlap windows for the synchronous work that still needs it.

The principle survives: Agile values working software over comprehensive documentation — but in a distributed team, documentation is what makes working software possible. The trade-off shifts; the values don't.

9o. Documentation and knowledge management

Agile does not mean no documentation. What it means: document what earns its keep.

  • Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) — one page per significant decision: the context, the decision, the alternatives rejected.
  • Runbooks — operational procedures for known scenarios (see §9h).
  • API docs and onboarding guides — the surfaces new engineers touch first.
  • Docs as code — documentation lives in the repository, reviewed like code, kept next to what it describes.
  • Living documentation — generated or maintained alongside the code so it stays true; dead docs are worse than no docs.

The test for any documentation: would a future engineer (or your future self, in three months) be lost without it? If yes, it earns its place.

9p. Product outcome orientation — shipping is not success

A mature process asks not only "did we deliver?" but "did this improve the outcome?"

  • Outcomes vs outputs — an output is a feature shipped; an outcome is a change in user behavior or business results. Process optimizes delivery; product thinking optimizes outcome.
  • OKRs and KPIs — connect team work to measurable objectives; a backlog item that doesn't move a metric should be questioned.
  • Adoption metrics and A/B testing — the empirical loop applied to product decisions: ship a variant, measure, keep what wins.
  • Discovery experiments — validate the problem before building the solution (see The Rhythm's model-first instinct at the product scale).

This is the value bucket from §9f made real: a fast process that ships the wrong thing is still a failure — just a faster one.

9q. Retirement and decommissioning

The SDLC runs from birth to retirement, but most phase lists stop at Maintenance. The last phase matters:

  • Decommission — shut the system down deliberately, not by neglect.
  • Archive data — preserve records that have legal or historical value.
  • Migrate users — move active users to the replacement before cutting the old system off.
  • Remove access — revoke credentials; an un-decommissioned system is a security liability.
  • Shutdown notice and migration plan — tell stakeholders, document the plan, preserve required records.

Retirement is a phase like any other: it deserves planning, a Definition of Done, and the same discipline as building the system did.

9r. Regulated and safety-critical environments

The rest of this doc mostly assumes product/startup/web development. In regulated, safety-critical, or high-compliance environments (medical devices, aviation, banking, government), the SDLC tightens:

  • Formal requirements traceability — every requirement linked to its test and its release evidence.
  • Change approval and audit trails — who approved what, when, recorded and reviewable.
  • Validation evidence — proof the system does what it claims, collected and retained.
  • Controlled releases — release gates, sign-off, and documented approval before production.

Agile can still be used — but it often becomes hybrid: iterative development inside, formal gates at the boundaries. The constraint isn't an excuse to abandon Agile; it's an additional constraint the process must serve.

9s. Worked example — a Kanban support flow end-to-end

The Scrum sprint walked through in §9 shows timeboxed, push-based work. Kanban is the other common shape — let's trace a single support ticket through a real Kanban board, to make the flow practices (§5) concrete.

  ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌────────┐
  │  TO DO   │ → │ TRIAGE   │ → │ IN WORK  │ → │ CODE     │ → │ DONE   │
  │  (WIP∞)  │   │ (WIP:5)  │   │ WIP: 3   │   │ REVIEW   │   │ (WIP∞) │
  │  ⑦   ⑤   │   │  ③  ① ⑨ │   │ (WIP:1)  │   │ (WIP:2)  │   │  ④ ③ ⑥ │
  │  ⑪       │   │          │   │  ③       │   │  ②       │   │        │
  └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └──────────┘   └────────┘

(Each numbered card is a ticket; the WIP limit under each column name says how many cards may sit there at once. Three columns have WIP limits — Triage 5, In Work 1, Code Review 2 — which is where the bottleneck will show.)

  1. Ticket arrives (To Do). A bug report from support, a one-line user email, a Slack ping. Everything lands here regardless of priority.
  2. Triage — the first pull. A maintainer pulls the top card when Triage has room (limit 5). They classify it: is this a genuine bug, a broken edge case, duplicated with another ticket, or a "works as designed"? They add a severity and route it onward.
  3. In Work — the WIP limit does the real work. Because only one card may be "In Work", the developer finishing a fix must either continue or stop — they can't hoard four in-progress tasks. When they finish card ③ and mark done, they pull the next triaged card. That column staying almost empty is itself a signal: the bottleneck is upstream (in Triage) or downstream (Code Review backing up).
  4. Done. The card sits in Done only long enough to be verified — deployed, confirmed by the reporter or the monitoring, closed. Nothing accumulates in Done; that would mean nobody is owning the finished work.

The board is the whole process: it is the state of the work. If the "Code Review" column fills to its limit of 2, a bottleneck is visible before it becomes a crisis — that's the inspection-and-adaptation loop in visual form. The ticket moved not on a scheduled sprint but as capacity, pull by pull — which is exactly the difference from Scrum's timeboxed push.

Contrast with the Scrum example in §9: there the batch is chosen in Sprint Planning and locked for two weeks; here the priority can change at any time, work arrives continuously, and there are no sprint reviews because the flow is constantly visible. Same SDLC phases underneath (design the fix, implement, test, deploy, maintain) — different navigation.

10. Summary — the process checklist

Next time you join a team or start a project, use this checklist to understand the process you're operating within:

QuestionIf you can't answer…
What methodology does the team use? (Scrum, Kanban, Scrumban, Waterfall?)Ask the Scrum Master or tech lead. Most teams are Scrumban (hybrid) without knowing it.
What's the cadence? (2-week sprints? continuous flow?)Check the project management tool (Jira, GitHub Projects). Look for sprint boundaries or a Kanban board.
What are the ceremonies? (stand-up, planning, review, retrospective?)Check the calendar. If there's no retrospective, the team isn't inspecting-and-adapting — that's a red flag.
Who's the Product Owner? Who prioritizes the backlog?If nobody owns the backlog, priorities will shift daily and the team will be reactive. A missing PO = a rudderless team.
What's the definition of done? When is a feature "done"? Does it include quality and security?If there's no shared definition, features will be "mostly done" — which means not done. Include tests, security, and monitoring. Write it down, agree on it.
How does deployment work? (manual? CI/CD? how often?) How do you roll back?If deployment is manual and rare, the feedback loop is months. If nothing is reversible, every release is gambling. Advocate for CI/CD and a safe rollback (feature flags, canary, blue-green).
Is the team protecting the sprint? (no mid-sprint additions?)If stakeholders add work mid-sprint, the timebox is broken. Protect it — or switch to Kanban (which is designed for continuous flow).
Is there technical debt? Is refactoring prioritized?If every sprint is 100% new features and 0% refactoring, debt is accumulating. Advocate for ~20% refactoring time.
What metrics show the process is healthy? (lead time, deployment frequency, escaped defects?)Without metrics (§9f), problems are invisible until they surface as a crisis. Pick a handful of flow/delivery/quality signals and watch them.
How are production incidents handled? (alerting, on-call, postmortems?)If something breaks and nobody notices or debriefs, the SDLC stops at deploy. Operations (§9h) is part of the loop, not downstream of it.
How does user feedback return to the backlog?If shipped work never informs the plan, you're optimizing delivery toward the wrong target. Outcomes, not just outputs (§9p).
How does AI fit in? (Copilot for code? AI for testing? Building agents?) What's the AI usage policy?Understand whether you're in an AI-augmented SDLC (AI assists you) or an ADLC (you're building agents). Set explicit guardrails so AI output is treated as untrusted input (§9d). The evaluation skill matters in both.

The first rows are about understanding your team's process; the later ones about measuring it, keeping it safe, and connecting it to value — plus the frontier. Together, they tell you: how does software get built here, is it improving, and where is it heading?

The sharper 10-question checklist

  1. What methodology are we using?
  2. What is our cadence?
  3. What ceremonies do we have — and why?
  4. Who owns prioritization?
  5. What is our Definition of Done?
  6. How do we test and secure the work?
  7. How do we deploy and roll back?
  8. How do we handle incidents and maintenance?
  9. What metrics tell us the process is healthy?
  10. How does AI assist or change the process?
