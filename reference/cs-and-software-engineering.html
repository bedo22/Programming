Foundations of computation (1843–1936) 1843 Lovelace, the first published algorithm Ada Lovelace wrote the first published algorithm for Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine — a procedure to compute Bernoulli numbers. She recognized that a general-purpose machine could go beyond arithmetic to process any manipulation of symbols. Widely credited as the world's first programmer (Wikipedia). 1931 Gödel's incompleteness theorems Kurt Gödel proved that any sufficiently powerful formal system is either incomplete or inconsistent — some true statements cannot be proven within it. This set the boundary question Turing soon turned into a theory of computation: what can a mechanical procedure know? (Wikipedia). The machine era (1936–1968) 1936 Turing, "On Computable Numbers" Alan Turing defined the Turing machine — a mathematical model of computation. Proved that some problems are provably unsolvable (the halting problem). This is the founding moment of CS: before any computer existed, Turing defined what computation means (Wikipedia). 1945 von Neumann architecture John von Neumann's "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC" set out the stored-program design: instructions and data held in one shared memory, fetched and executed sequentially. Nearly every computer since — including today's — follows this architecture (Wikipedia). 1947 The transistor John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley demonstrated the transistor at Bell Labs — a solid-state switch far smaller, faster, and more reliable than vacuum tubes. It made practical, miniaturized, mass-produced computing possible (Wikipedia). 1948 Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" Claude Shannon founded information theory — the mathematical study of quantifying, storing, and communicating information. The bit became the unit. This underpins all data compression, encryption, and transmission (Wikipedia). 1950 Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" Turing proposed the imitation game — the "Turing test": a machine "thinks" if a human cannot reliably distinguish its answers from another human's. The first operational definition of machine intelligence, and the seed of the AI branch of the discipline (Wikipedia). 1956 Dartmouth workshop — "artificial intelligence" is coined John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and Nathaniel Rochester organized the Dartmouth conference, coining the term "artificial intelligence" and defining the field's program: make machines use language, form abstractions, and solve problems reserved for humans (Wikipedia). 1957 FORTRAN — the first widely used high-level language IBM's Backus team delivered FORTRAN, translating math-like notation into efficient machine code. It proved programmers need not write assembly — a compiler could do the hard work — launching the high-level-language era (Wikipedia). 1958 LISP — the first language of AI John McCarthy designed LISP at MIT — the first language built around symbolic, list-based processing and recursive functions. It became the language of early AI research for decades and introduced garbage collection (Wikipedia). 1968 Simula — object orientation is born Ole-Johan Dahl and Kristen Nygaard designed Simula at the Norwegian Computing Center — the first language with classes, objects, and inheritance. Smalltalk consolidated the paradigm, but Simula is its origin; virtually all mainstream OOP (C++, Java, C#, Python) descends from its ideas (Wikipedia). 1968 Knuth, "The Art of Computer Programming" Vol. 1 Donald Knuth published the first volume of his monumental work. He coined "analysis of algorithms" — the formal study of the time and space resources algorithms consume (Wikipedia). Big O notation, originally from mathematics (Bachmann 1894, Landau), was adopted by Knuth for classifying algorithm efficiency. This is the birth of solution evaluation as a formal discipline. 1968 NATO Software Engineering Conference (Garmisch, Germany) The "software crisis" was declared: projects were running over budget, over schedule, with poor quality. The conference named "software engineering" as a discipline, arguing that software should be engineered like bridges — with rigor, methodology, and accountability. This is the founding moment of SE as distinct from CS (Wikipedia). 1968 Dijkstra, "Go To Statement Considered Harmful" Edsger Dijkstra argued that the goto statement makes code unmaintainable. This launched structured programming — the idea that code should have clear control flow. The first engineering principle about code quality, not just correctness. The software & internet era (1969–2004) 1969 ARPANET — packet switching The ARPANET sent its first packets between research machines, demonstrating packet switching: data is split into independently routed packets and reassembled at the destination. The seed of the internet and of networked, distributed computing (Wikipedia). 1969 UNIX Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie built UNIX — a small, portable, multi-user operating system. Its design philosophy (small composable tools, everything-is-a-file, plain text) shaped nearly every later operating system and the culture of C and open source (Wikipedia). 1970 Codd, "A Relational Model of Data" Edgar F. Codd proposed the relational model for databases: data as tables of rows, manipulated with set-based relational algebra instead of programmatic navigation. The root of SQL, PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle — most stored and retrieved data still follows it (Wikipedia). 1972 C — the systems language Dennis Ritchie wrote C to re-implement UNIX portably — a small, fast, close-to-the-metal language whose descendants (C++, Java, C#, Go, Rust) dominate systems and web programming to this day (Wikipedia). 1974 SQL (SEQUEL) Donald Chamberlin and Raymond Boyce defined SQL at IBM: a declarative query language over the relational model. You say what you want, not how to get it — the language that opened data manipulation to a wide audience (Wikipedia). 1975 Brooks, "The Mythical Man-Month" Fred Brooks's law: "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." The first rigorous study of software project management. Key insight: communication overhead grows quadratically with team size. SE is not just about code — it's about people and coordination. 1981 IBM Personal Computer (PC) IBM released the IBM Personal Computer (model 5150) with the Intel 8088 CPU, MS-DOS, and open architecture. It sparked the PC revolution, spawned the Wintel alliance, and made personal computing affordable and ubiquitous. 1983 TCP/IP — the internet is born The ARPANET adopted TCP/IP, letting separate networks interconnect into a network of networks. IP addresses packets, TCP guarantees delivery — together they remain the plumbing of the modern internet (Wikipedia). 1984 Apple Macintosh (Lisa-based) Apple introduced the Macintosh — the first widely available graphical user interface (GUI) with mouse, windows, icons, and desktop metaphor. It shifted computing from command-line to visual and influenced every OS since. 1985 GNU / the free-software movement Richard Stallman launched the GNU project to build a complete, free Unix, and defined copyleft (the GPL). This seeded the culture that bred Linux, Git, and every open-source community the industry now runs on (Wikipedia). 1986 Brooks, "No Silver Bullet" Brooks argued there is no single advance yielding an order-of-magnitude leap in productivity because software's essential complexity is irreducible. A standing, sobering antidote to every "revolutionary" tool — including today's AI claims (Wikipedia). 1991 Linux Linus Torvalds released the Linux kernel as open source. Combined with GNU's tools it became the dominant kernel of the web and cloud — nearly every cloud instance and Android device runs Linux (Wikipedia). 1991 ISO/IEC 9126 The first international standard for software quality evaluation. Defined 6 quality characteristics: functionality, reliability, usability, efficiency, maintainability, portability. This formalized "what makes a solution good" — the parameters of evaluation (Wikipedia). 1993 Mosaic / the World Wide Web goes public Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web (HTML + HTTP, at CERN since 1989) and NCSA Mosaic's graphical browser made the internet an accessible mass medium — the platform onto which the discipline's center of gravity moved (Wikipedia). 1994 Gang of Four, "Design Patterns" Gamma, Helm, Johnson, Vlissides cataloged 23 design patterns — reusable, named solutions to common OO design problems. The book gave teams a shared vocabulary of proven design. 1995 Java — "write once, run anywhere" Sun released Java: a portable, garbage-collected, object-oriented language compiled to bytecode running on a virtual machine. It brought portability to enterprises and later Android, and taught a generation how VMs and platforms shape code (Wikipedia). 1995 JavaScript Brendan Eich wrote JavaScript in ten days to make pages interactive in the browser. Against all odds it became the language of the whole Web and, with Node, the server — the most deployed language on earth (Wikipedia). The agile, cloud & big-data era (1997–2017) 1997 Deep Blue beats Kasparov IBM's Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov — the first time a machine beat a reigning champion in a match. A landmark for the symbolic ("search + evaluation") AI, and a caution about how AI headlines differ from engineering reality (Wikipedia). 1998 Google — search at web scale Larry Page and Sergey Brin made search actually work at web scale (PageRank ranked pages by the links pointing at them). Their commodity clusters and later big-data tooling became the template for distributed systems (Wikipedia). 1999 Extreme Programming (XP) Kent Beck described XP — test-first development, pair programming, continuous integration, small releases. These turned agile principles into daily engineering practice and are the ancestors of TDD and modern CI (Wikipedia). 1999 Hunt & Thomas, "The Pragmatic Programmer" Introduced DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself), rubber duck debugging, and the pragmatic mindset. The engineering principles as practice, not theory. 2000 Robert C. Martin, SOLID The five principles — Single Responsibility, Open-Closed, Liskov Substitution, Interface Segregation, Dependency Inversion — made canonical by Martin's writings (the acronym by Michael Feathers ~2004). The default design checklist for OO systems (Wikipedia). 2001 Agile Manifesto 17 practitioners replaced contract-heavy process: value individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration, and responding to change over process, documentation, contracts, and following a plan. Agile popularized iterative, pragmatic software (Wikipedia). 2004 MapReduce Google's Dean and Ghemawat offered a clean abstraction for clusters: map transforms run in parallel across machines, then reduce aggregates. The primitive that made "big data" tractable on commodity hardware (Wikipedia). 2005 Git — distributed version control Linus Torvalds built a distributed version control system for the Linux kernel: history replicated to every clone, cheap branching and merging. It became the substrate of collaboration, GitHub, and CI/CD (Wikipedia). 2006 AWS — cloud computing (S3 & EC2) Amazon launched S3 (March) and EC2 (August), one of the first public infrastructure-as-a-service platforms. Compute and storage became rentable by the minute instead of bought as hardware — scalability (§1c) stopped being exotic and became a daily lease (Wikipedia). 2009 Node.js Ryan Dahl built Node: JavaScript on the server, on V8, with a non-blocking event loop — highly concurrent with a single thread. It unified the front- and back-end language and brought JS to the server (Wikipedia). 2011 ISO/IEC 25010 (SQuaRE) Superseded ISO 9126. Expanded from 6 to 8 quality characteristics: functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security, maintainability, portability. The modern standard for "what makes a solution good" (Wikipedia). 2012 AlexNet — the deep-learning breakthrough Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton trained a deep convolutional network on GPUs and won ImageNet 2012 by a wide margin. It proved that deep neural networks, large datasets, and GPU compute together beat hand-crafted features — the founding moment of modern AI and the seed of everything that follows in this list (Wikipedia). 2013 Docker — containers for everyone Solomon Hykes open-sourced Docker, wrapping Linux containers (cgroups + namespaces) in a developer-friendly interface. An app and its dependencies could now ship as one immutable image that runs identically anywhere. Containers solved the "works on my machine" problem and became the deployment unit of the cloud era (Wikipedia). 2014 Kubernetes — container orchestration Google open-sourced Kubernetes, a cluster manager inspired by its internal Borg system. It made deploying, scaling, and healing containerized services declarative. Kubernetes became the de-facto orchestrator — the operating system of the data center — and seeded the Cloud Native Computing Foundation (Wikipedia). The AI era (2017–present) 2017 Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" — the Transformer Google researchers introduced the Transformer, a network architecture based entirely on attention, dispensing with recurrence. It trained faster and generalized further than anything before. Nearly all mainstream modern large language models — GPT, Claude, Gemini — are Transformer descendants (arXiv:1706.03762). 2018 GPT / BERT — pre-training at scale OpenAI's GPT and Google's BERT pioneered pre-training: train once on vast text, then adapt or few-shot for specific tasks. This transfer learning turned the Transformer into a general tool and set the scaling trajectory of today's frontier models (Wikipedia). 2021 GitHub Copilot — AI-assisted coding GitHub and OpenAI released Copilot (technical preview, June 2021), an AI that autocompletes and generates code inside the IDE. For the first time, AI wrote code at the developer's elbow. It reframed the job: less typing, more reviewing — the evaluation skill this doc teaches (§4–§7) became the bottleneck, and the "treat AI output as untrusted" guardrail (§9d) was relevant from day one (Wikipedia). 2021 Log4Shell (CVE-2021-44228) A critical remote-code-execution flaw in Apache Log4j, a ubiquitous Java logging library, disclosed December 2021. Trivial to exploit and widely embedded across enterprise environments, it made software supply-chain security a first-class concern: the code you didn't write (your dependencies) became your biggest risk (Wikipedia). 2022 ChatGPT — LLMs reach the public OpenAI released ChatGPT (November 30, 2022), putting a large language model in front of 100 million users in two months. AI was no longer a research artifact — it was a general-purpose tool people used daily. It marks the moment the discipline's center of gravity shifted toward building with and for AI (OpenAI). 2023 GPT-4 — the multimodal jump GPT-4 (March 2023) read images, passed advanced exams, and produced robust, deployable code — the moment baseline generative coding became solid enough that the developer's skill moved from typing toward judging (Wikipedia). 2024 o1 — reasoning models OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) spends compute before answering — chain-of-thought at scale — gaining large accuracy on math and programming. This added a new axis, inference-time reasoning, beyond pre-training size (Wikipedia). 2024 EU AI Act The first comprehensive AI regulation entered into force (1 August 2024) with a risk-based framework and, for foundation models, GPAI obligations phasing in through 2026–2027. Law now shapes the discipline's boundaries (Wikipedia). 2024 MCP (Model Context Protocol) Anthropic open-sourced MCP, an open standard for connecting a model to tools and context consistently — the "USB-C for AI tools" the agent ecosystem rallied around (Wikipedia). 2025 DeepSeek R1 — open-weights reasoning DeepSeek released (January 2025) a reasoning model with frontier-level capability published open-weights — evidence that capable reasoning can be efficient and inspectable, correcting the "closed, costly race" narrative (Wikipedia). 2026 Frontier agents & regulation in force Frontier is now agentic: models drive systems that act on tasks with tools (Claude Opus 5, Gemini 3.5 Flash), and the EU AI Act's GPAI requirements took effect February 2026. Judgment — deciding what to trust a model with — is the discipline's sharpest skill (§9).
