## Overall verdict

This is a **strong, well-scoped doc** that fills a genuine gap on the shelf: the engineering of money movement. The "instructions not money" definition, the auth → capture → settlement pipeline, the webhook-as-source-of-truth principle, and the payment state machine are all genuinely useful mental models that most engineering curricula skip entirely.

The duplication boundaries are well-drawn. The doc correctly defers webhooks/transactions/idempotency machinery to Backend Engineering, PCI/fraud/secrets to Security & Threat Modeling, orders/invoices/ledger rows to Data Modeling, WooCommerce vs. Shopify to WordPress & CMS, and freelancer income to Freelance Web Practice. It stays in its lane: the money-specific state machine, failure cases, and integration patterns.

The main gaps are in **international/multi-currency payments**, **regulatory depth beyond PCI**, **subscription lifecycle edge cases**, **payment testing discipline**, **reconciliation mechanics**, and **payment analytics**. These are the areas where a developer who has read this doc would still be surprised in production.

# 1. What the document does especially well

## 1.1 The core definition is excellent

> You never move money. You move instructions to move money.

This single sentence is the most important insight in the doc. It explains every surprising behavior in payments: the settlement lag, the reversal rules, the fees, the fraud surface, and the regulation. Every developer who builds a payment feature needs to internalize this before writing a line of code.

## 1.2 The three terms are clearly defined

Authorization, capture, and settlement are the three verbs every payment developer must know without flinching. The doc defines each one precisely and explains the temporal gap between them. The "promise until settlement" mental model follows directly.

## 1.3 The money-movement parties table is comprehensive

The table mapping merchant, customer, issuing bank, acquiring bank, card networks, and PSPs — with "whose side they're on" — is genuinely useful. Most developers don't know the difference between an issuing bank and an acquiring bank, and that gap causes confusion when debugging payment failures.

## 1.4 The five invariants are well-chosen

The five invariants (money is data until settlement, every leg can fail, idempotency is the contract, webhook is source of truth, fraud follows value) compress the entire discipline into rules a developer can check against. This is the kind of thing that should be printed and pinned to a wall.

## 1.5 The payment state machine is well-designed

The state machine (created → requires_action → processing → failed/succeeded → captured → settled → refunded/disputed) is correct and comprehensive. The distinction between "we saw a success page but no succeeded event" as the classic bug is exactly the kind of production wisdom that separates this doc from a tutorial.

## 1.6 The integration patterns are well-structured with decision rules

The three patterns (hosted checkout, embedded elements, API-direct) each have a clear "when it wins" and the decision rule ("hosted checkout unless you have a reason") is correct. The PCI scope implications for each pattern are stated explicitly.

## 1.7 The worked example is concrete and complete

The small SaaS example walks from product creation through webhook handling to the first crisis. It covers test mode, reconciliation, and the "which layer is yours" lesson. This is well-done.

# 3. Completeness gaps

## 3.1 Missing: Multi-currency and international payments

This is the largest gap in the doc. A global product will encounter multi-currency payments immediately, and the doc doesn't cover:

**How to store amounts:**
- Integer minor units (cents/pence) vs. decimal
- Why floating-point is forbidden for money
- Currency code storage (ISO 4217)
- Amount + currency as an inseparable pair

**Currency conversion:**
- When conversion happens (at authorization, at capture, at settlement)
- Who sets the exchange rate (PSP, card network, issuing bank)
- Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC) and why it's usually a bad deal for the customer
- FX fees and who pays them

**Cross-border payments:**
- Cross-border fees (card networks charge extra for cross-border transactions)
- Settlement currency vs. transaction currency
- How PSPs handle multi-currency settlement

**Local payment methods by geography:**
- The doc lists regional leaders (Paystack, Mercado Pago, Razorpay) but doesn't explain the local payment methods they support:
  - UPI (India) — instant bank-to-bank, no card needed
  - PIX (Brazil) — instant, QR-code based
  - iDEAL (Netherlands) — bank redirect
  - Bancontact (Belgium) — debit card scheme
  - Sofort/Klarna (Germany/EU) — bank redirect
  - Alipay/WeChat Pay (China) — QR-code wallets
  - Konbini (Japan) — convenience store payment

**When to prioritize local methods:**
- The decision rule should be: "your customers' payment habits are the requirement, not your favorite dashboard"
- In many markets, cards are a minority payment method

Add a section:

> **Multi-currency and international payments**
> 
> Store amounts as integer minor units with an ISO 4217 currency code. Never use floating-point for money. The amount-currency pair is inseparable — a "100" without a currency is a bug.
> 
> Currency conversion happens at different points depending on the PSP and the card network. The customer's issuing bank may apply its own FX rate on top of the network rate. Cross-border transactions incur additional fees from the card networks.
> 
> Local payment methods are not optional in most markets. In India, UPI dominates. In Brazil, PIX dominates. In the Netherlands, iDEAL dominates. Your PSP's country/payment-method coverage is a first-class requirement.

## 3.2 Missing: Regulatory landscape beyond PCI

The doc mentions PCI, KYC, and money-transmission law briefly but doesn't cover the regulatory landscape that governs payment products:

| Regulation | What it governs | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| PCI DSS | Card data security | Already covered in the doc |
| PSD2/PSD3 (EU) | Payment services, open banking, SCA | Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) mandates 3DS for most EU card transactions; open banking enables A2A payments |
| AML/KYC | Anti-money laundering, know your customer | Platforms that move other people's money must verify identities |
| Sanctions screening | OFAC, EU sanctions lists | Every payment must be screened against sanctions lists |
| Sales tax/VAT/GST | Tax on digital goods and services | Where tax applies, registration thresholds, calculation services |
| Money transmitter licensing (US) | State-by-state licensing for money movement | Platforms holding or routing customer money may need licenses in every US state |
| BNPL regulation | Consumer credit, disclosure requirements | BNPL providers face increasing regulation |
| Open banking (PSD2, UK OB) | Bank data access, payment initiation | Enables A2A payments; regulated API access to bank accounts |

Add a section:

> **Regulatory landscape**
> 
> PCI DSS is the card-industry security standard, but it is not the only regulation that governs payment products. PSD2/PSD3 mandates Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) for most EU card transactions, which is why 3DS is triggered for European customers. Open banking regulations enable A2A payments. AML/KYC obligations apply to platforms that move other people's money. Sales tax/VAT/GST on digital goods is a rule surface that changes by jurisdiction.
> 
> The lazy rule: let a service own the regulatory updates. Stripe Tax, Paddle (merchant of record), and Quaderno exist because tax and compliance are moving targets. Building your own tax engine is a product, not a feature.

## 3.3 Missing: Subscription lifecycle edge cases

The doc covers dunning, proration, and usage billing, but doesn't cover the edge cases that every subscription product encounters:

**Trial → paid conversion:**
- When does the trial end? (exact timestamp vs. end-of-day)
- What happens if the card on file fails at conversion?
- Does the user get a grace period?
- Is the first charge prorated?

**Cancellation timing:**
- Immediate cancellation vs. end-of-period cancellation
- What happens to the user's access after cancellation?
- How do you handle "cancel and refund the unused portion"?

**Pause/hold:**
- Can a user pause their subscription?
- How does pausing affect the billing cycle?
- What happens to usage during a pause?

**Plan migration:**
- Upgrade mid-cycle: prorate the difference, charge immediately
- Downgrade mid-cycle: credit the difference, apply at next cycle
- What happens to features the user loses on downgrade?

**Grandfathering:**
- When you change pricing, what happens to existing subscribers?
- Do they keep their old price forever? Until they change plans?
- How do you communicate the change?

**Free tier → paid tier:**
- Is the free tier a separate plan or a subscription with $0 price?
- What triggers the upgrade prompt?
- How do you handle the transition without losing the user's data?

Add a section:

> **Subscription lifecycle edge cases**
> 
> The subscription state machine in the doc covers the happy path. The edge cases are where the bugs live:
> 
> - Trial conversion: if the card fails at conversion, the user should get a grace period with a clear "update your card" prompt, not an immediate lockout.
> - Cancellation: decide upfront whether cancellation is immediate or end-of-period. The customer's expectation and your revenue recognition depend on this choice.
> - Plan migration: upgrades prorate immediately; downgrades credit at next cycle. Document this in the pricing page.
> - Grandfathering: when you change pricing, existing subscribers keep their price until they change plans. Communicate this clearly.

## 3.4 Missing: Payment testing discipline

The doc mentions test mode and test cards but doesn't cover the testing discipline:

**What to test:**
- Successful payment (test card 4242…)
- Declined payment (test card 4000 0000 0000 0002)
- 3DS challenge (test card that triggers 3DS)
- 3DS failure (test card that fails 3DS)
- Expired card
- Insufficient funds
- Incorrect CVC
- Processing timeout
- Webhook delivery failure and retry
- Duplicate webhook delivery
- Out-of-order webhook delivery

**How to test webhooks:**
- Use a webhook testing tool (Stripe CLI, webhook.site, or a local tunnel)
- Verify signature verification works
- Verify idempotent handling (send the same event twice)
- Verify out-of-order handling (send a later event before an earlier one)

**How to test subscriptions:**
- Use test clocks (Stripe test clocks) to simulate time passing
- Test trial → paid conversion
- Test failed renewal → dunning → cancellation
- Test proration on plan change

**How to test failed payments:**
- Use test cards that simulate specific failure modes
- Verify the failure is recorded in your state machine
- Verify the customer sees the right error message
- Verify the retry logic works

Add a section:

> **Testing payments**
> 
> Test mode is where you learn every failure before a customer pays you. The test cards are free education:
> 
> - 4242 4242 4242 4242 — success
> - 4000 0000 0000 0002 — declined
> - 4000 0027 6000 3184 — 3DS challenge required
> - 4000 0000 0000 9995 — insufficient funds
> - 4000 0000 0000 0069 — expired card
> 
> Test the webhook path explicitly: verify signatures, send duplicate events, send events out of order. The handler must be idempotent and order-tolerant.
> 
> For subscriptions, use test clocks to simulate time passing. Test the full lifecycle: trial → active → past_due → canceled.

## 3.5 Missing: Reconciliation mechanics

The doc mentions reconciliation as a habit but doesn't explain what reconciliation actually checks:

**What reconciliation checks:**
- Every payment your app thinks succeeded matches a payment the PSP thinks succeeded
- Every payout the PSP made matches your expected revenue
- Every refund your app issued matches a refund the PSP processed
- Every chargeback the PSP reported matches your records
- The amounts match (including fees)

**How to handle mismatches:**
- App says succeeded, PSP says failed → the webhook was missed; update your state
- PSP says succeeded, app says pending → the webhook was missed; update your state
- Amount mismatch → check for FX conversion, fees, or partial refunds
- Missing payment → check for a webhook delivery failure

**Automated vs. manual reconciliation:**
- Automated: a scheduled job compares your payment records against the PSP's API
- Manual: a human reviews the PSP dashboard and your accounting records
- The lazy rule: automate the comparison, escalate the mismatches

**Reconciliation frequency:**
- Daily for high-volume products
- Weekly for low-volume products
- After every payout for marketplaces

Add a section:

> **Reconciliation mechanics**
> 
> Reconciliation is the question "did our records match what the provider settled?" Concretely:
> 
> 1. Pull the PSP's settlement report (or query the API for all payments in the period).
> 2. Compare against your payment records: every payment, refund, chargeback, and payout.
> 3. Flag mismatches: missing payments, amount discrepancies, status disagreements.
> 4. Resolve: most mismatches are missed webhooks. Update your state from the PSP's record.
> 
> The weekly script is the minimum. The audit trail is a product, not a chore.

## 3.6 Missing: Payment analytics and reporting

The doc doesn't cover the metrics that tell you whether your payment system is healthy:

| Metric | What it measures | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Authorization rate | % of payment attempts that are authorized | Low authorization rate = lost revenue; diagnose by card network, country, payment method |
| Decline rate | % of payment attempts that are declined | High decline rate = friction; diagnose by decline reason code |
| Chargeback rate | % of successful payments that are disputed | High chargeback rate = processing risk; card networks may terminate your account |
| Conversion rate | % of checkout sessions that complete a payment | Low conversion = checkout friction; diagnose by step in the funnel |
| Retry success rate | % of failed payments that succeed on retry | Low retry success = the retry logic isn't working |
| Dunning recovery rate | % of past_due subscriptions that are recovered | Low recovery = the dunning emails aren't working |
| Revenue per transaction | Average transaction value | Trend indicator for pricing and product changes |

**Payment funnel analysis:**
- Checkout started → payment method entered → payment submitted → payment authorized → payment captured → payment settled
- Each step has a drop-off rate. The biggest drop-off is where to focus.

**Diagnosing payment failures:**
- Decline reason codes (from the PSP) tell you why a payment failed
- Common reasons: insufficient funds, expired card, incorrect CVC, do not honor (generic), lost/stolen card
- "Do not honor" is the most common and least actionable — it means the issuing bank said no without explaining why

Add a section:

> **Payment analytics**
> 
> The metrics that tell you whether your payment system is healthy:
> 
> - Authorization rate: % of attempts authorized. Low = lost revenue. Diagnose by card network, country, payment method.
> - Decline rate: % of attempts declined. High = friction. Diagnose by decline reason code.
> - Chargeback rate: % of successful payments disputed. High = processing risk.
> - Conversion rate: % of checkout sessions that complete. Low = checkout friction.
> 
> The payment funnel (checkout started → payment submitted → authorized → captured → settled) has a drop-off rate at each step. The biggest drop-off is where to focus.

## 3.7 Missing: 3D Secure in depth

The doc mentions 3DS as a fraud defense but doesn't explain how it works:

**What 3DS is:**
- A protocol where the customer authenticates with their issuing bank during checkout
- The bank may show a challenge (OTP, biometric, app confirmation) or approve silently (frictionless flow)
- PSD2/SCA mandates 3DS for most EU card transactions

**When 3DS is triggered:**
- The PSP's risk engine decides whether to request 3DS
- High-risk transactions (high amount, new card, cross-border) are more likely to trigger 3DS
- The merchant can request 3DS explicitly

**Liability shift:**
- Without 3DS: the merchant is liable for fraud chargebacks
- With 3DS: the issuing bank is liable for fraud chargebacks (in most cases)
- This is why 3DS reduces fraud liability

**Friction vs. security tradeoff:**
- 3DS adds friction to checkout (the customer must authenticate)
- Frictionless 3DS (no challenge) minimizes friction
- The PSP's risk engine balances friction and security

Add to the fraud section:

> **3D Secure (3DS)**
> 
> 3DS is the protocol where the customer authenticates with their issuing bank during checkout. The bank may show a challenge (OTP, biometric, app confirmation) or approve silently (frictionless flow). PSD2/SCA mandates 3DS for most EU card transactions.
> 
> The liability shift is the key: with 3DS, the issuing bank is liable for fraud chargebacks in most cases. Without 3DS, the merchant is liable. This is why 3DS reduces fraud liability — but it adds checkout friction. The PSP's risk engine balances the tradeoff.

## 3.8 Missing: Stored cards and vaulting

The doc doesn't cover how to store payment methods for future use:

**Customer-initiated vs. merchant-initiated transactions:**
- Customer-initiated (CIT): the customer is present and authorizes the transaction (e.g., a one-time purchase)
- Merchant-initiated (MIT): the merchant charges a stored card without the customer present (e.g., a subscription renewal)
- Card networks have different rules for CIT vs. MIT

**Card network rules for stored credentials:**
- The customer must explicitly consent to storing their card
- The merchant must identify the transaction as MIT and reference the original CIT
- The card network may require additional authentication for high-risk MITs

**Card updater / account updater services:**
- When a card expires or is replaced, the PSP can automatically update the stored card details
- This reduces failed renewals due to expired cards
- Stripe, Braintree, and Adyen all offer card updater services

Add a section:

> **Stored cards and vaulting**
> 
> Storing a card for future use (subscriptions, one-click checkout) requires the customer's explicit consent. The stored card is a token, not the PAN — the PSP vaults the card and gives you a token.
> 
> Card networks distinguish customer-initiated transactions (CIT, the customer is present) from merchant-initiated transactions (MIT, the merchant charges a stored card). MITs must reference the original CIT and may require additional authentication for high-risk transactions.
> 
> Card updater services automatically update stored card details when a card expires or is replaced. This reduces failed renewals. Enable it.

## 3.9 Missing: Merchant of Record (MoR) model

The doc mentions Paddle/Lemon Squeezy as MoR but doesn't explain what MoR means:

**What MoR means legally:**
- The MoR is the legal seller of the product
- The MoR handles sales tax/VAT/GST calculation and remittance
- The MoR handles refunds and chargebacks
- The MoR is responsible for compliance (PCI, tax, consumer protection)

**What you give up:**
- The MoR takes a larger fee (typically 5%+ vs. 2.9% for a PSP)
- You lose some control over the checkout experience
- The MoR's name appears on the customer's bank statement

**When MoR is worth it:**
- You sell globally and don't want to handle tax registration in every jurisdiction
- You don't want to handle PCI compliance
- You don't want to handle refunds and chargebacks
- You're a small team and the fee premium is worth the operational simplicity

Add to the ecosystem section:

> **Merchant of Record (MoR)**
> 
> A Merchant of Record (Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, FastSpring) is the legal seller of your product. The MoR handles sales tax/VAT/GST calculation and remittance, refunds, chargebacks, and PCI compliance. You pay a larger fee (typically 5%+ vs. 2.9% for a PSP) but offload the entire compliance and payment operations surface.
> 
> MoR is worth it when: you sell globally, you're a small team, and the fee premium is less than the cost of hiring a tax/compliance person. MoR is not worth it when: you have high volume (the fee compounds), you need full checkout control, or you already have tax/compliance infrastructure.

## 3.10 Missing: Sales tax / VAT / GST on digital goods

The doc mentions tax in the ecosystem table but doesn't explain the rule surface:

**Where tax applies:**
- Generally, tax applies based on the customer's location (destination-based)
- For digital goods, the customer's location is determined by billing address, IP address, or card issuing country
- Different jurisdictions have different rules (US sales tax is state-by-state; EU VAT is country-by-country)

**Registration thresholds:**
- Many jurisdictions have thresholds below which you don't need to register
- In the US, economic nexus thresholds vary by state
- In the EU, the One-Stop Shop (OSS) simplifies VAT registration

**Tax calculation services:**
- Stripe Tax, Quaderno, Avalara, TaxJar
- These services calculate the correct tax rate based on the customer's location and the product type
- They also handle tax registration and filing in some cases

**Tax-inclusive vs. tax-exclusive pricing:**
- Tax-inclusive: the displayed price includes tax (common in EU)
- Tax-exclusive: the displayed price excludes tax, tax is added at checkout (common in US)
- The choice affects your pricing page and checkout UX

Add a section:

> **Sales tax / VAT / GST on digital goods**
> 
> Tax on digital goods is a rule surface that changes by jurisdiction. The general rule: tax applies based on the customer's location (destination-based). For digital goods, the customer's location is determined by billing address, IP address, or card issuing country.
> 
> The lazy rule: let a service own the tax calculation. Stripe Tax, Quaderno, and Avalara calculate the correct rate based on the customer's location and product type. Building your own tax engine is a product, not a feature.
> 
> Tax-inclusive pricing (EU) vs. tax-exclusive pricing (US) affects your pricing page and checkout UX. Decide upfront and document the choice.

## 3.12 Missing: Double-entry bookkeeping for payment records

The doc doesn't cover how to store payment records in a way that supports accounting:

**Why double-entry matters:**
- Every payment has two sides: the customer's payment and your revenue
- Every refund has two sides: the customer's refund and your revenue reduction
- Every fee has two sides: the PSP's fee and your expense
- Double-entry bookkeeping ensures the books balance

**How to store payment records:**
- A `payments` table with: amount, currency, status, PSP payment ID, created_at, settled_at
- A `ledger` table with: entry_id, payment_id, account (revenue, fees, refunds), amount, currency, created_at
- Every payment creates at least two ledger entries (revenue + fees)
- Every refund creates at least two ledger entries (revenue reduction + fee refund)

Add a brief mention:

> **Payment records and the ledger**
> 
> Every payment has two sides: the customer's payment and your revenue. Every refund has two sides. Every fee has two sides. Store payment records in a way that supports double-entry bookkeeping: a `payments` table for the payment lifecycle, a `ledger` table for the accounting entries. Every payment creates at least two ledger entries. The ledger is what your accountant reconciles against the bank statement.

| No payment analytics | You don't know your authorization rate or decline rate | Track authorization rate, decline rate, chargeback rate, conversion rate |

# 11. Bottom line

This is a **strong doc** that fills a genuine gap on the shelf. The "instructions not money" definition, the auth → capture → settlement pipeline, the webhook-as-source-of-truth principle, and the payment state machine are all genuinely useful mental models. The duplication boundaries are well-drawn. The worked example is concrete. The failure modes are comprehensive. The ecosystem catalog is thorough.

The main gaps are in **international/multi-currency payments**, **payment testing discipline**, **reconciliation mechanics**, **payment analytics**, **3D Secure depth**, **subscription edge cases**, and **regulatory landscape beyond PCI**. These are the areas where a developer who has read this doc would still be surprised in production.

The doc's central insight — "know which layer is yours" — is the right framing. The PSP handles the banking; the bank handles the money; the regulator handles the rules; the developer handles the state machine, the webhooks, and the reconciliation. The developer who understands this boundary will build payment features that don't lose money, don't double-charge, and don't get terminated by the PSP.

## Final recommendation

**Strong draft, publishable with additions.** The core mental models are correct and well-explained. Add the five "must add" sections (multi-currency, payment testing, reconciliation mechanics, payment analytics, 3DS depth) and the doc becomes a complete reference for the money layer of the shelf.

Print the summary checklist. Run it before you ship any money-moving feature. The whole discipline compresses to: know which layer is yours. The rest is the provider's, the bank's, and the regulator's — priced into your fees.
