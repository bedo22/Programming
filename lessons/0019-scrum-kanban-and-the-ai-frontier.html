1. Scrum in practice — 3 roles, 5 events, 3 artifacts

Scrum is the most widely adopted Agile framework. Here's what you'll actually encounter at work:

  • 3 roles: Product Owner (owns the backlog, decides priorities), Developers (build the increment, self-managing), Scrum Master (removes impediments, facilitates, serves the team).
  • 5 events: The Sprint (2-week container), Sprint Planning (what + how), Daily Scrum (15-min sync), Sprint Review (demo to stakeholders), Sprint Retrospective (inspect the process itself).
  • 3 artifacts: Product Backlog (everything that could be built, prioritized), Sprint Backlog (what's committed this sprint), the Increment (the usable product at sprint end).

The sprint retrospective is the most important ceremony — it's Manifesto principle 12 ("reflect and adjust") in action. It's metacognition (L13) at the team level: inspect your own process, decide what to change. If your team doesn't do retrospectives, or if they produce no changes, you're doing cargo cult Agile (§3 below).

2. Kanban — flow, not timeboxes

Kanban is the alternative to Scrum's sprint model. Where Scrum uses fixed 2-week timeboxes, Kanban uses continuous flow — work is pulled as capacity permits. The 6 practices: visualize the workflow (a board), limit WIP (don't start more than N items), manage flow, make policies explicit, implement feedback loops, improve collaboratively.

The key difference: Scrum's constraint is the timebox (finish in 2 weeks). Kanban's constraint is the WIP limit (don't start more than 3 items). Scrum is push-based (commit to a batch at planning); Kanban is pull-based (pull work as capacity permits). Most teams use Scrumban — loose sprint cadence with Kanban's WIP limits. This is what you'll most likely encounter.

3. The common failure modes — what goes wrong

Failure modeWhat happens
Cargo cult AgileThe team does the ceremonies but not the values. Stand-ups are status reports, retrospectives produce no changes, sprints are mini-waterfalls. Form without function.
Velocity as a metricManagement uses story points to measure productivity. Teams inflate estimates to look faster. Velocity becomes a target, not a measure.
Technical debt accumulationEvery sprint is 100% new features, 0% refactoring. Code quality degrades; velocity drops to near zero. The debt crisis.
Developer burnoutSustainable pace (principle #8) is ignored. The team sprints at 110% every sprint. After 3 months, developers quit.

The fix for all of these: read the Manifesto. Ask "why are we doing this ceremony?" If the answer is "because Scrum says so," you're doing cargo cult. Each ceremony exists for a reason — understand it. And advocate for ~20% refactoring time every sprint. Technical debt is a loan — it compounds.

4. The two AI frontiers — AI-augmented SDLC vs Agent Development Lifecycle

AI is changing the process in two distinct ways. Understanding the difference is critical:

  • AI-augmented SDLC: AI assists the human in building traditional software. Copilot writes code, AI generates tests, AI drafts requirements. Not a new lifecycle — the same SDLC with the human shifting from writer to evaluator. The evaluation skill (L17) becomes the premium skill.
  • Agent Development Lifecycle (ADLC): A genuinely new lifecycle for building AI agents — autonomous systems that pursue goals, use tools, take actions. The agent IS the product. 5 phases: Agent Design (LLM, tools, memory, orchestration) → Build (prompt engineering, tool definitions) → Evaluate (goal achievement, hallucination, safety) → Deploy with guardrails (action limits, human-in-the-loop) → Monitor for drift (hallucination, behavioral drift, prompt injection).

The key distinction: AI-augmented SDLC is AI helping you build traditional software faster. ADLC is building AI agents as a new type of software. The first accelerates the existing process; the second is a new process for a new type of product. The ADLC is too young (2024–present) for a standalone reference — it's a chapter in the bigger story. But it's the chapter you'll be living in.
