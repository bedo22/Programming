Upstream of Code: How Products Discover Problems

Design Thinking — the discipline of figuring out what problem is worth solving before deciding how to build it. The desirability/feasibility/viability Venn, the five phases, and why "build the right thing" is the move that prevents building the wrong thing perfectly. Anchored to Design Thinking.

Where this fits

Lesson 20 covered the interface people touch (the human layer's surface). This lesson goes one layer further upstream — before anyone touches code, before the interface exists, before system design (L11). This is the layer that answers "what problem is worth solving, and for whom?" For 20 lessons you've learned how to build well; this is where you learn to build the right thing. The Human/product movement concludes with L22 (Income/Markets).

1. The three circles — the Venn every method serves

Every Design Thinking method answers the same question — is this product in the intersection of three circles? (IDEO, ~1998):

  • Desirability — Do people actually want this? (Human-centered: real needs, observed, not assumed.)
  • Feasibility — Can we build it with our tech and skills? (Technical.)
  • Viability — Does it make business sense? (It must be sustainable.)

Design Thinking lives in the intersection. If any circle is empty, the product fails — and each failure looks different:

  • Desirable + feasible but not viable → a beloved product that loses money and dies.
  • Desirable + viable but not feasible → vaporware you can't ship.
  • Feasible + viable but not desirable → "a solution in search of a problem" — you built the wrong thing perfectly.

That last failure mode is exactly what the rest of the journey has been engineering against — and Design Thinking is the layer whose job is to prevent it.

2. What Design Thinking is — and what it is not

It is a disciplined approach to problem discovery and framing before solution construction. It treats the problem as unknown and discoverable, not given. Its currency is empathy (direct observation of users), abductive reasoning (inferring the best explanation for observed behavior), and iteration (cycles that refine the problem definition, not just the solution). Its artifacts are insight statements, experience maps, and testable prototypes — not code.

It is not any of the things it's usually confused with:

Confused withWhat that really isThe difference
Visual / UI designTypography, color, layout, interactionDesign Thinking discovers the problem; UI design renders the solution (L20).
UX researchA research phase/disciplineResearch is Design Thinking's tool, not its whole.
A design sprintA 5-day eventThe sprint is one recipe within it, not the discipline.
AgileA delivery processAgile (L18) delivers fast; Design Thinking decides what to deliver. They pair end-to-end.

This is the same boundary logic as L11 (System Design) and L7 (The Rhythm): each layer owns one question. Design Thinking owns "what problem, for whom?"

3. The five phases — Stanford d.school, and the design sprint

The most widely taught recipe is Stanford's d.school five phases. They're a rhythm (you loop back out of order), not a strict waterfall:

PhaseWhat you do
EmpathizeObserve and interview real users. The goal: understand their needs and context — not your assumptions about them. This is the empathy engine that feeds everything.
DefineFrame the problem as a point-of-view statement: "a user needs X so that Y." The way you frame it determines what solutions are imaginable.
IdeateGenerate many solutions — divergent thinking first, then converge. Quantity over quality at this stage.
PrototypeBuild something cheap and testable — a sketch, a clickable mock, a paper cutout. Prototypes make ideas concrete enough to test (L20's interface becomes testable).
TestShow users the prototype. Watch what they do, not what they say. Feed learnings back into an earlier phase.

Google Ventures' Design Sprint (Jake Knapp, 2016) compresses these into one 5-day week — Understand → Sketch → Decide → Prototype → Test — with a cross-functional team and real users on Friday. It's the "bake the whole loop in 5 days" recipe, and it's how you'll most likely experience Design Thinking in a real job.

4. The failure mode — design theater

The most important caution in this discipline (also the hardest lesson to remember, because it quietly infects everything):

  • Design theater — following the method without the empathy. Post-it notes, journey maps, and workshops that produce no insight and no change. It's "a solution in search of a problem" replayed at the process level: the ritual without the goal.
  • Critics (Natasha Jen's "Design Thinking is Bullshit," Don Norman's "Rethinking Design Thinking") attacked exactly this: when it becomes a corporate ritual, empathy drains out and it's theater.
  • The tell: prototypes get produced but never tested with real users; insight statements never change what gets built; the exercise ends, the backlog doesn't change.

The guard: Design Thinking's output is a decision, not a deliverable. If a sprint ends and the team isn't more sure what to build (or sure it shouldn't build it), it was theater. This is the one failure mode that reverses the whole point of being upstream.

The win

  
You now hold the upstream layer: Design Thinking decides what problem is worth solving, for whom — before any code, before UI (L20), before system design (L11). The three circles (desirable, feasible, viable) frame every product decision, and the empty-circle failure modes explain why great software can still fail. The five phases (Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test) are a rhythm, and the one guardrail to never forget: design theater — method without empathy — is how you quietly build the wrong thing. This completes the stack: Design Thinking (what) → System (how it fits) → Rhythm (how I build it) → UI/UX (how people use it).
