1. Two disciplines, one name

You've been practicing two disciplines without knowing their names. They share a foundation but have different goals, different success criteria, and different mindsets:

  • Computer Science — the science of computation. What can be computed? How efficiently? What are the theoretical limits? It's a science: it discovers what's possible and proves theorems. Founded by Alan Turing in 1936.
  • Software Engineering — the engineering of software. How do we build reliable, maintainable systems at scale? How do we make trade-offs under real constraints? It's an engineering discipline: it builds things, makes trade-offs, and is judged by outcomes. Named at the 1968 NATO conference in response to the "software crisis."

The key difference: CS asks "is it correct and efficient?" SE asks "is it reliable, maintainable, and shipped?" HackerRank optimizes for CS (solve the problem, minimize Big O). Your React job optimizes for SE (build the feature, make it maintainable, ship it). Both use JavaScript. Both use algorithms. But they're judged by different criteria — and knowing the difference explains why they feel different.

2. The 90-year lineage — two threads that braid

YearEventWhat it contributed
1936Turing, "On Computable Numbers"Defined the Turing machine. Proved some problems are provably unsolvable (the halting problem). The founding moment of CS — before any computer existed.
1968Knuth, "The Art of Computer Programming"Coined "analysis of algorithms" — the formal study of time and space complexity. Big O notation adopted for CS. The birth of solution evaluation.
1968NATO Software Engineering ConferenceThe "software crisis" declared. Named "software engineering" as a discipline. The founding moment of SE.
1975Brooks, "The Mythical Man-Month""Adding manpower to a late project makes it later." SE is about people and coordination, not just code.
1999Hunt & Thomas, "The Pragmatic Programmer"DRY, rubber duck debugging. The engineering principles as practice.
2000Robert C. Martin, SOLID principlesFive design principles for maintainable code. The acronym coined by Michael Feathers (~2004).
2011ISO/IEC 25010The international standard for software quality: 8 characteristics. The formal framework for "what makes a solution good."

The two threads braid: the science thread (Turing → Knuth) gave us the measures (Big O). The engineering thread (NATO → Dijkstra → Brooks → SOLID) gave us the parameters (quality attributes) and the principles (DRY, SOLID, KISS). The judgment — which parameters matter when — is the skill that sits at the braid. Lesson 17 teaches it.

3. The engineering principles — the constants that survive everything

These principles have survived from Knuth (1968) to the React Compiler (2024). They're the heuristics that guide trade-off decisions:

  • KISS — Keep It Simple, Stupid. Origin: Kelly Johnson, Lockheed (1960s). The simplest solution is usually the most maintainable, testable, and fastest to ship. This is The Rhythm's "do the simplest thing that could possibly work" (L7).
  • YAGNI — You Aren't Gonna Need It. Origin: Kent Beck, XP (1999). Don't build flexibility you don't need yet. This is The Rhythm's "defer decisions until you need them" (L15).
  • DRY — Don't Repeat Yourself. Origin: Hunt & Thomas (1999). Every piece of knowledge has a single authoritative representation. But: DRY is about knowledge duplication, not code duplication — two similar functions with different business rules should NOT be merged.
  • SOLID — five principles for maintainable design: Single Responsibility (one job per module), Open-Closed (extend, don't edit), Liskov Substitution (subtypes must work), Interface Segregation (small interfaces), Dependency Inversion (depend on abstractions). Origin: Robert C. Martin (2000).
