Problem-Solving — Computational Thinking + Heuristics

Pólya's four phases tell you when to think (Understand → Plan → Execute → Look back). This lesson gives you what to do within those phases: the four pillars of computational thinking (the lens), Pólya's seven heuristics (the moves), and Schoenfeld's missing fifth element (metacognition). Anchored to Problem-Solving.

Where this fits

Lesson 12 gave you Pólya's four phases — the universal meta-rhythm. But Pólya tells you when to understand, plan, execute, and review. He doesn't tell you how to understand or how to devise a plan when you're stuck. This lesson fills that gap: the lens (computational thinking), the moves (heuristics), and the monitor (metacognition).

The relationship in one sentence: Pólya gives you the when (the four-phase rhythm); computational thinking gives you the what (the four moves you make within those phases); heuristics give you the how (the specific strategies to try); metacognition gives you the monitor (checking if it's working). Together they form a complete problem-solving method.

1. The lens — computational thinking's four pillars

If Pólya's four phases are the rhythm, computational thinking is the lens — the way you see and structure the problem during Phases 1 and 2. The term was coined by Seymour Papert in 1980 (the creator of Logo) and brought to prominence by Jeannette Wing's 2006 essay in Communications of the ACM, which argued that "thinking computationally is a fundamental skill for everyone, not just computer scientists."

Wing defined computational thinking as "the thought processes involved in formulating problems so their solutions can be represented as computational steps and algorithms." It has four pillars — the moves you make while understanding and planning:

  • Decomposition — break the problem into smaller, manageable parts. ("Count word frequencies" → (a) split text, (b) normalize each word, (c) count in a map.)
  • Pattern recognition — identify patterns in the problem or similarities to problems you've solved before. ("I need to count things → a hash map is the right structure.")
  • Abstraction — focus on essential features, ignore irrelevant detail. ("A word is a lowercased, punctuation-stripped token split by whitespace.")
  • Algorithm design — develop the step-by-step solution. (The numbered steps you write before coding.)

Your HackerRank comment used all four. You decomposed (split, normalize, count), recognized the pattern (hash map for counting), abstracted ("word" = lowercased + stripped token), and designed the algorithm (the numbered steps). You were doing computational thinking without knowing its name.

2. The moves — Pólya's heuristic dictionary

Heuristics are rules of thumb that usually help but don't guarantee a solution. An algorithm guarantees a result; a heuristic usually finds one faster. Pólya's book contains a "short dictionary of heuristics" — the strategies you try during Phase 2 (Devise a plan) when you're stuck. Here are the seven most useful for programmers:

HeuristicThe question it answers
Analogy"Have I seen a similar problem? Can I use its method?"
Specialization"Can I solve a specific case first, then generalize?"
Working backwards"What would the answer look like? What produces that?"
Decomposition"Can I break it into parts, solve each, then combine?"
Variation"Can I restate the problem to make it easier?"
Auxiliary elements"Can I introduce something extra that helps?"
Generalization"Can I solve a more general problem that includes this one?"

The meta-heuristic: when stuck, cycle through these. Try analogy first (is this like something I know?). Then specialization (let me try a concrete example). Then working backwards (what does the answer look like?). Then decomposition (can I break it apart?). Most problems yield to one of these within a few minutes of cycling.

3. The monitor — Schoenfeld's missing fifth element

In the 1980s, Alan Schoenfeld (UC Berkeley) did something important: he tested Pólya's strategies empirically. His finding, published in Mathematical Problem Solving (1985), was a landmark:

Schoenfeld's finding: Pólya's heuristics alone are weak. Students who learned the strategies still failed on non-routine problems. The strategies need two things Pólya didn't emphasize enough: domain-specific tactics (you need to know the techniques, not just the meta-strategy) and metacognition — the act of monitoring your own thinking and adjusting as you work.

Metacognition is thinking about your own thinking while you're thinking. In practice, it means asking yourself every few minutes:

  • "Am I making progress, or am I spinning?" — If you've been stuck for 10 minutes on the same approach, stop. Try a different heuristic. Or re-read the problem.
  • "Is this approach worth continuing?" — Sunk cost is a trap. If brute force works but is too slow, don't keep optimizing it — step back and consider a different paradigm.
  • "Should I go back to Phase 1?" — Sometimes your plan fails because your understanding was wrong.
  • "What am I actually trying to do right now?" — It's easy to drift into solving a different problem than the one asked.

The practical takeaway: Pólya's four phases + computational thinking's four pillars + the seven heuristics are the method. Metacognition is the monitor on top of the method — the loop that checks if the method is working. Without it, you can cycle through phases and heuristics mechanically and still fail, because you never stopped to notice you were on the wrong track.
